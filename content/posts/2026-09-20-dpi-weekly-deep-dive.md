# DPI Weekly Deep Dive — The Rate Card Lands: UPI's 0.4% MDR Becomes the Law of the Rails | Week of September 14–20, 2026

## Executive Summary

For most of 2026, the single biggest open question in Indian payments infrastructure has been *whether* UPI would get a merchant fee, and *at what rate*. This week the question closed. On **September 15**, the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) notified its Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) framework: **0.4% on person-to-merchant (P2M) UPI transactions above ₹2,000**, effective **October 15, 2026**, with a ₹300 cap on transactions of ₹75,000 and above, a flat ₹5 fee in sectors such as railways, telecom, insurance, fuel and agricultural inputs, and full exemption for person-to-person transfers and small merchants. Consumers pay nothing; surcharging — a merchant passing the fee to the customer — is expressly prohibited, as is any platform fee levied by UPI apps. NPCI framed the levy as funding "infrastructure resilience, innovation, cybersecurity and customer service" for a rail that processed **2,451 crore transactions worth ₹29.9 lakh crore in August alone**.

The notification is the third act of a story this newsletter has tracked since spring: the **Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026** (introduced in the Lok Sabha on August 4) rewrote Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 to give the government legal room to allow charges; a Parliamentary Standing Committee report in March had already called for MDR on large merchants; and August's industry consultations produced rate expectations of 25–40 basis points. What nobody fully priced in was the political detonation. Within hours, the fee was framed as *foreign policy*: the opposition tied it to the USTR's 2026 National Trade Estimate — which flagged US card networks' exclusion from UPI — prompting the Finance Ministry's Department of Financial Services (DFS) to issue an unusually sharp rebuttal calling the external-pressure claim "**patently false and misleading**," and pointing out that the circular permits credit transactions on UPI *only* via RuPay.

The takeaway: India has chosen a third path between the two classic models of instant-payment economics. Brazil's Pix leaves merchant pricing entirely to banks (averaging ~0.22% of transaction value, per BIS economists); card networks charge 1.5–2%+. India has instead published a **regulated, capped, exemption-heavy rate card** — high enough to fund the rails, low enough to keep 95%+ of merchant transactions (by volume) free, and blunt-edged enough (₹300 cap, flat ₹5 sectors) to avoid punishing high-value payments. The experiment now runs in public: if pass-through shows up in prices, or small merchants find creative ways to be re-classified, October 15 is only the beginning of the argument, not the end.

## The Story in Depth

### Context

Zero-MDR on UPI has been policy since **January 2020**, when the government mandated nil merchant fees on the young rail to accelerate adoption — a subsidy decision that worked spectacularly for volume and catastrophically for unit economics. UPI grew from a few hundred million monthly transactions to **2.45 billion in August 2026**, while the banks, PSPs and apps running it recovered costs through cross-subsidy, lending attach, and advertising. The government's own incentive scheme — payments to banks for low-value UPI transactions — has been shrinking in relative terms: **₹1,922.77 crore in FY25, ₹2,196.21 crore in FY26, and a Budget estimate of ₹2,000 crore for FY27**, against a system now clearing nearly ₹30 lakh crore a month.

The policy system spent 2025 in denial and 2026 in motion. In 2025, the Finance Ministry dismissed MDR speculation as "false, baseless and misleading." In March 2026, the **Parliamentary Standing Committee** broke ranks and recommended restoring MDR on large merchants. On **August 4, 2026**, the government introduced the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, which amends **Section 10A of the PSS Act, 2007** — the provision that had barred banks and system providers from charging users of prescribed payment modes — so that the Centre can *notify* which modes must remain free, opening legal space for charges on the rest. By September, Reuters reported NPCI meeting lenders and payment firms to settle the mechanics. This newsletter's August deep dive ("The Great UPI Pricing Reckoning") catalogued the spectrum of expectations: 25–40 basis points, ₹13,500–22,000 crore annual revenue pools, and a devolution fight over who keeps what. What remained unknown was the threshold, the cap, and the political weather.

### What Happened This Week

**The rate card (September 15).** NPCI published its MDR circular along with a **42-question FAQ** that reads, unusually, like an attempt to pre-empt every WhatsApp rumour in advance. The essentials: **0.4% MDR on standard P2M UPI transactions above ₹2,000**; a **₹300 cap** where the transaction is ₹75,000 or more; a **flat ₹5** for specified sectors — railways, telecom, insurance, fuel and agricultural inputs; **P2P transfers remain free regardless of amount**; small merchants (the P2PM category) stay outside the framework; and the existing **₹2,000-and-below nil-MDR protection** continues for everything else. Above-threshold transactions are roughly **4% of UPI merchant volume but 67% of its value** (Jefferies), and NPCI itself noted that **more than 95% of merchant payment volume is ₹2,000 or below**. Effective date: **October 15, 2026** — one month of what NPCI called "adequate lead time to update their software engines and billing systems." Two prohibitions carry the consumer-protection weight: merchants **cannot pass MDR on to customers**, and apps **cannot add platform fees** on top. A **dedicated fund for promoting UPI among small merchants**, seeded with **5% of total MDR collections**, accompanies the framework.

**The money moves (September 15–16).** Markets repriced within a day. **Paytm and One Mobikwik shares jumped up to 5%** on the announcement, with brokerages modelling the revenue uplift: **Bernstein** estimates a 40 bps MDR applied to half of UPI merchant payments by value could generate a **₹22,000 crore annual revenue pool by FY28**; earlier banker estimates at 25–30 bps ran **₹13,500–16,000 crore**. Bank of America called Paytm "the key beneficiary from UPI," sizing a **₹11,500 crore** monetisation pivot, while noting the stock had already run **18%** on MDR news flow — "a large part of this upside is mainly captured." The devolution expectations follow card-economics logic: issuing banks take the bulk (industry veterans peg interchange at **60–80%** of MDR; SBI alone is estimated to earn **up to ₹3,000 crore** a year, with Bank of Baroda and HDFC Bank around ₹800 crore each), **PSP banks ~10%**, and acquirers/apps the remaining **15–20%** — a split that structurally favours PhonePe, Google Pay and Paytm, who lock in the most lucrative acquiring terms, over smaller apps with weaker negotiating hands.

**The political storm (September 15–19).** Rahul Gandhi framed the fee as capitulation — "Modi has again surrendered to US" — linking it to the **USTR's 2026 National Trade Estimate**, which had raised two grievances: US electronic payment suppliers' inability to participate in UPI (including credit transactions) on a level playing field with RuPay, and NPCI's **30% market-share cap on third-party app providers** (announced November 2020, enforcement date December 2026). The government's counter was two-pronged. First, "your own MPs backed it" — the Standing Committee's March recommendation. Second, and more substantively, the **DFS took to X** (September 17–19) to rebut the foreign-pressure narrative point by point: the September 15 circular "does not allow credit transactions on UPI by any other credit card other than the RuPay credit card"; the MDR will "provide a self-sustaining revenue model to smaller companies to compete for higher share in the UPI ecosystem"; and "the allegation that MDR has been introduced under any external influence is patently false and misleading." The Finance Ministry separately said it does **not expect MDR to push users back to cash** and that the burden on consumers would be **monitored** — a commitment worth holding it to.

### Why It Matters

This is the first time India has attached a regulated price to the country's most-used digital infrastructure in its ten-year history, and the design choices reveal the actual theory of the thing. The ₹2,000 threshold is a demographic boundary: below it — the overwhelming majority of transactions by count — the informal economy keeps its free rail. Above it, where the value concentrates, the formal economy pays for resilience. The **anti-steering rules** (no surcharging, no platform fees) mean the merchant absorbs the cost invisibly, which protects consumer sentiment but embeds the fee in retail prices — the classic MDR trade-off, now on a public rail. The **5% small-merchant fund** is a redistribution mechanism: money collected from larger merchants subsidising acceptance infrastructure for smaller ones, a rare explicit cross-subsidy written into a rate notification.

Strategically, the timing is not accidental. UPI's costs — switching, fraud control, cybersecurity, dispute resolution — are scaling with volume, and the government's incentive budget was never going to grow at ₹30 lakh crore per month. A self-funding rail also changes India's export posture: countries weighing UPI adoption (and the BRICS DPI conversation of last week's summit) can now see a monetisation model that does not depend on perpetual Indian budget lines. And the December 2026 enforcement of the 30% TPAP cap, arriving eight weeks after monetisation begins, means the two most structural changes to UPI's market shape in a decade will land in the same quarter.

## Technical Deep Dive

The rate card's mechanics are worth stating precisely, because most coverage flattened them:

| Transaction type | MDR from October 15, 2026 |
| --- | --- |
| P2P transfers (any amount) | Nil |
| P2M, ₹2,000 or below | Nil |
| P2M, ₹2,000.01–₹74,999 | 0.4% of transaction value |
| P2M, ₹75,000 and above | ₹300 cap (0.4% up to ₹75,000, effective rate declines above) |
| Small merchants (P2PM category) | Nil |
| Railways, telecom, insurance, fuel, agri inputs (above ₹2,000) | Flat ₹5 |
| RuPay credit card on UPI (above ₹2,000) | Existing regime (~1.1–2%) unchanged |
| Consumers | Nil, and surcharging prohibited |

Arithmetically, the design produces a regressive-then-regressive-again curve: a ₹2,500 payment costs the merchant ₹10 (0.4%), a ₹50,000 payment ₹200 (0.4%), a ₹1 lakh payment ₹300 (0.3%), a ₹10 lakh payment ₹300 (0.03%). The cap makes UPI *the cheapest rail in the ecosystem for high-ticket payments* — a direct challenge to card economics at exactly the transaction sizes (electronics, travel, insurance premia) where cards earn their keep.

In the transaction flow, the 0.4% is collected by the **acquiring side** (the merchant's bank or aggregator) and devolved along the four-party model — acquirer, issuer, payer PSP, TPAP/app — with NPCI's steering-committee-set shares expected to mirror pre-2020 conventions (issuer-heavy, ~70% interchange historically). Implementation burden lands on merchant systems: billing engines, payment gateway fee-splitting logic, reconciliation reports, and the merchant-facing dashboards where MDR deductions must now appear. One month is tight for enterprise ERP integration at scale; expect go-live friction and a grace-period enforcement posture through November.

The boundary cases matter more than the headline. RuPay **credit** on UPI keeps its separate, higher interchange — meaning instrument choice now has a visible price signal (bank-account UPI at 0.4% vs credit-on-UPI at 1.1–2%+). PPI/wallet-funded UPI above ₹2,000 already carried interchange and continues. And **AutoPay mandates, Reserve Pay and EMI structures** — the plumbing of agentic and subscription commerce — will need explicit treatment in follow-up circulars, because recurring debits above ₹2,000 are about to become a merchant-cost planning exercise.

## Government Perspective

The official position has four planks. First, **sustainability**: the fee funds "infrastructure resilience, innovation, cybersecurity and customer service" without new budget outlays, replacing a shrinking incentive scheme (₹2,000 crore FY27 estimate) with a market mechanism. Second, **competition**: DFS's argument that MDR gives "smaller companies" a self-sustaining revenue model to challenge the big three apps — the counterintuitive claim that monetisation *de-concentrates* the market, since revenue-hungry challengers finally get paid for the transactions they win. Third, **sovereignty**: only RuPay credit cards on UPI; no foreign network advantage; the USTR narrative rejected outright. Fourth, **protection**: the ₹2,000 carve-out, the surcharging ban, the small-merchant fund, and the ministry's stated intent to monitor consumer burden.

The legislative pathway is the quiet constitutional story: rather than NPCI acting alone, the government first amended the PSS Act (August 4 Bill) to clarify who may be charged and who is protected, then let the regulator's NPCI publish the rate card. Whether the **devolution formula** and the **small-merchant fund's governance** receive the same legislative transparency — or arrive as steering-committee circulars — is the next accountability test. The friction with Washington is now a standing feature: UPI joins Pix in the set of public payment rails that have become trade-dispute exhibits, and the DFS's defiant post suggests New Delhi intends to wear that as a badge, not an embarrassment.

## Citizen Impact

For the consumer paying by UPI, nothing changes on October 15 — the app shows the same screen, debits the same account, and any attempt to add a "MDR surcharge" line is prohibited (and worth reporting to the bank's and NPCI's grievance channels). The effects are second-order. **Prices**: merchants above the threshold will, over time, fold the fee into pricing — a ₹20 MDR on a ₹5,000 appliance purchase is margin, not surcharge. **Small merchants** — the kirana, the chai stall, the street vendor — stay free, and the 5% fund is meant to widen their acceptance infrastructure. **Sector payments** get simpler: the flat ₹5 on railways and fuel replaces percentage anxiety for high-value tickets (a ₹5,000 fuel fill-up costs ₹5, not ₹20). **High-value payers** benefit from the ₹300 cap, which caps the merchant's incentive to steer customers to cards or cash at large ticket sizes.

The risks to watch: threshold **creep** (will ₹2,000 become the new ₹1,000 in future revisions?); **reclassification pressure** on the small-merchant boundary as the ₹2,000+ transaction becomes visibly costlier to accept; and **instrument steering** — merchants nudging customers away from RuPay-credit-on-UPI (1.1–2%) toward account UPI (0.4%), which is consumer-friendly but changes credit-access patterns. The Finance Ministry's monitoring pledge should be made concrete: published data on MDR collections, pass-through indicators, and small-merchant acceptance growth — the payments-stat transparency this ecosystem's oversight deserves.

## Global Context

The cleanest comparison remains **Brazil's Pix**, the other state-built instant rail at global scale. Pix is free for individuals, but Brazil left merchant pricing to banks — BIS economists measured an average merchant cost of **0.22%** of transaction value, versus 2.2% on credit cards. India has now done the opposite: regulated *up* (0.4% published, capped, sector-flat) rather than leaving it to bank pricing power. The trade-offs are philosophical: Brazil's model is flexible but opaque; India's is transparent but rigid. Both rails now share another distinction — they are exhibits in US trade policy (Pix was cited in the 2026 tariff dispute; UPI in the USTR National Trade Estimate), confirming that public payment infrastructure has become a geopolitical file, not just a domestic one.

Against card networks' global norm of 1.5–3% MDR, UPI's 0.4%-capped regime is aggressively cheap for merchants even after monetisation — which is precisely the point. The rail that runs 2.45 billion monthly transactions at a regulated 0.4% ceiling, free below ₹2,000, is a cost floor under India's digital economy and a benchmark every other instant-payment system (and every negotiating card network) will now be measured against. Singapore's SAFR, China's agent-payment guidelines and the Visa–Mastercard–Ant "Know Your Agent" stack may dominate the governance headlines from last week's fest; the rate card is the quieter export.

## Looking Ahead

Five markers for the coming weeks: **(1) October 15 go-live** — watch for integration glitches, grace-period enforcement, and the first MDR lines on merchant settlement reports. **(2) The devolution disclosure** — whether NPCI publishes the acquirer/issuer/PSP/app split as transparently as the rate itself. **(3) December 2026** — enforcement of the 30% TPAP market-share cap, landing eight weeks after monetisation, reshaping the PhonePe–GPay duopoly question. **(4) The small-merchant fund's first disbursements** — 5% of collections, governance unspecified; a transparency opportunity or a slush-fund risk. **(5) Budget 2027** — whether the central subsidy line shrinks as promised, completing the shift from fiscal support to fee-funded infrastructure. And beneath all of it, the threshold: ₹2,000 is this year's answer. Whether it survives next year's review is the single best indicator of whether this was infrastructure funding — or the first turn of a ratchet.
