## From the Memoirs of a Future Citizen

It was India's 80th Independence Day. Prime Minister Modi stood on the ramparts of the Red Fort and made an announcement that every middle-class parent had been waiting for: free online coaching for competitive examinations.

The crowd cheered. PhysicsWallah offered "unconditional support." The media ran headlines about easing financial burdens on families who spent lakhs on coaching factories. And buried in the fine print — though "fine" is doing a lot of work for print that most people would never see — was the mechanism: India's Digital Public Infrastructure.

The same DPI that was "voluntary" when Aadhaar launched in 2009. The same DPI that became mandatory for bank accounts, SIM cards, GST registration, midday meals, and school attendance. The same DPI that was about to become the gateway for your child's exam preparation.

In the language of Indian governance, "voluntary" has always meant: "We haven't built the mandate yet. Give us six months."

---

## The Education DPI: When Free Means You Pay With Data

The announcement was carefully crafted. PM Modi told the nation: *"We have digital public infrastructure, and we have highly talented teachers and educators. By bringing those massive resources together, we are going to build an entire robust network to give absolutely free coaching to the youth."*

What he didn't say was that this "robust network" would sit on top of Aadhaar-authenticated profiles, DigiLocker-verified academic records, and Account Aggregator-linked financial data to determine "eligibility." Because nothing says "free education" like a multi-layered data extraction pipeline to verify that you deserve it.

The PIB factsheet released the same week celebrated 145 crore Aadhaar numbers, 798 crore UMANG transactions, and 90 lakh monthly ONDC transactions. The subtext was clear: India's DPI was no longer infrastructure. It was a operating system. And every new service — from coaching to commerce to credit — was just another app running on top of a platform you couldn't uninstall.

The coaching announcement was the latest in a long pattern. Aadhaar was voluntary. Then it was mandatory for subsidies. Then for bank accounts. Then for SIM cards. Then for school attendance. Now for competitive exam coaching. Each expansion came with the same justification: efficiency, inclusion, empowerment. And each expansion came with the same cost: another piece of your life funnelled into a system you didn't design, can't audit, and increasingly can't opt out of.

The middle-class families Modi was trying to help are the same families who had already submitted to Aadhaar-linked everything — their bank accounts, their tax filings, their children's school attendance. The free coaching wasn't a gift. It was the loyalty programme for a data subscription they'd already been paying for years.

## The RBI Governor's Paradox: AI for Inclusion That Excludes

Three days after the Red Fort speech, RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra stood at the FIBAC 2026 conference in Mumbai and delivered a warning that could have been a satire script itself.

*"India stands at a unique vantage point to leverage AI,"* he said. *"We have the most advanced public digital infrastructure, whether it is Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, ONDC, the Unified Lending Interface, the Account Aggregator."*

Then came the twist: *"Careless AI use could create new forms of financial exclusion and instability."*

Read that again. The man was standing on a platform built specifically to end financial exclusion, warning that building more things on that platform could create new financial exclusion. It was like a fire chief standing in a burning building announcing that the fire department's new equipment might cause more fires.

The Unified Lending Interface — ULI — was the centrepiece. Billed as "UPI for credit," it would standardise and simplify lending by letting financial institutions pull data from multiple sources: bank accounts, GST filings, land records, insurance. All through the Account Aggregator framework. All through "consent."

The problem, which Malhotra acknowledged but couldn't solve, was that AI lending models trained on DPI data inherit every bias baked into that data. If your Aadhaar-linked history doesn't include formal credit transactions — because you're rural, because you're unbanked, because you use cash — the AI doesn't see "no credit history." It sees "high risk." And it denies you the loan.

This is not a bug. This is the feature of building credit infrastructure on top of identity infrastructure. ULI promises to serve the underserved, but its data sources systematically exclude the people who need credit most. The farmer without GST filings. The daily wage worker without bank statements. The woman whose financial life exists entirely in the informal economy.

ULI was designed to reduce the ₹20-25 lakh crore MSME credit gap. But when the Deputy Governor Swaminathan J. had to remind lenders just months earlier to take an "empathetic approach towards MSMEs facing financial distress" — you knew the system was already failing the people it was built for. When you need a central banker to remind banks to be *empathetic*, the infrastructure isn't the problem. The incentives are.

## The Consent Theatre Gets a Standing Ovation

And then there was the Account Aggregator framework — the consent layer that made all of this possible.

A week earlier, SBI Managing Director Ashwini Kumar Tewari had delivered the most honest assessment of India's consent architecture ever spoken by a sitting bank official: *"Nobody understands the fine print. And they give a single consent for everything."*

He was describing the AA framework's single consent mechanism — the digital signature that lets a financial institution access your data across all services with one click. One consent for loans. One consent for wealth management. One consent for account opening. One consent for everything, read by nobody, understood by fewer.

The AA ecosystem had 17 account aggregators, 650 financial information users, and 150 financial information providers, collectively serving around 160 million accounts and handling 3.66 billion requests. By one count, over 2.2 billion financial accounts were now "enabled" for consent-based data sharing.

"Enabled" is doing a lot of heavy lifting. The SBI MD's confession — that customers "do not read the fine print before authorising and end up signing up without understanding" — was not a bug report. It was a feature description. The AA framework was designed for speed and scale, not comprehension. Consent was an artefact, not a conversation. A checkbox, not a choice.

In the same week, RBI was "in the process of introducing standards designed to improve… transparency and awareness in consent management and data sharing." Which is bureaucratic for: we built the consent system, deployed it to 160 million accounts, and now we're thinking about maybe making it understandable.

The global standard Tewari referenced was GDPR — Europe's data protection framework, which requires granular, informed, purpose-specific consent. India's AA framework, by contrast, offered a single consent artefact that covered everything from your bank balance to your GST returns. It was the difference between signing a specific contract and signing a blank page.

## ONDC: 200 Million Transactions and Counting (Sellers Not Included)

Meanwhile, the Open Network for Digital Commerce was celebrating a milestone: 200 million cumulative transactions. The first 100 million took 20 months. The next 100 million took six. By June 2026, ONDC had 20 crore buyers, 5 lakh sellers, presence in 1,000 cities, and nearly 90 lakh monthly transactions.

The headlines were triumphant. *"A paradigm shift in the way buyers and sellers are looking at digital commerce,"* declared ONDC CEO T Koshy. *"Breaking monopolies and creating an ecosystem where innovation thrives."*

Behind the press releases, small sellers told a different story. Technical barriers remained formidable. Onboarding processes required GST registration, digital catalog management, and integration with seller platforms — a trifecta of requirements that excluded the very informal retailers ONDC was supposed to empower. The gap between the platform's rhetoric of inclusion and its reality of digital gatekeeping was, by August 2026, a chasm.

A seller in Tier-3 city who had never filed GST didn't see "an open network empowering businesses of all sizes." They saw a digital wall with 12 steps, three registrations, and a YouTube tutorial they couldn't load on their 2G connection.

ONDC was DPI's answer to Amazon and Flipkart — and it was, technically, a more open architecture. But openness without accessibility is just a different kind of monopoly. Instead of being locked out of one platform, small sellers were locked out of seven.

## What Actually Happened This Week

**1. PM Modi announces free coaching via DPI.** On India's 80th Independence Day, the Prime Minister announced a nationwide free online coaching initiative for competitive examinations, leveraging India's Digital Public Infrastructure and "top educators." The PIB release framed it as alleviating financial burden on middle-class families, but the delivery mechanism — DPI-authenticated, Aadhaar-linked, digitally gated — raised familiar questions about whether "free" access was really "conditional" access.

**2. RBI Governor warns AI could create new financial exclusion.** At the FIBAC 2026 conference in Mumbai, RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra praised India's DPI stack — Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, ONDC, ULI, Account Aggregator — as a global advantage, then immediately warned that "careless AI use could create new forms of financial exclusion and instability." The ULI platform, designed to expand credit access to underserved segments, relies on data sources that systematically exclude the informal economy.

**3. SBI MD flags consent comprehension crisis.** SBI Managing Director Ashwini Kumar Tewari publicly acknowledged that Account Aggregator's single-consent mechanism means customers "do not read the fine print before authorising and end up signing up without understanding." He contrasted India's approach with GDPR's granular consent requirements. The AA framework serves 160 million accounts with 3.66 billion data requests.

**4. ONDC crosses 200 million transactions.** The Open Network for Digital Commerce announced surpassing 200 million cumulative transactions, with the second 100 million coming in just six months. The platform has 20 crore buyers and 5 lakh sellers across 1,000 cities. However, onboarding barriers — GST registration requirements, digital catalog management, platform integration — continue to exclude informal retailers the network was designed to empower.

**5. PIB celebrates 12 years of Digital India.** The government's factsheet catalogued the scale of DPI deployment: 145 crore Aadhaar numbers (up from 0.42 crore in 2010-11), 798 crore UMANG transactions, 90 lakh monthly ONDC transactions, and 5.16 lakh Common Service Centres. The same week, concerns about consent comprehension, financial exclusion, and digital barriers to small sellers revealed the gap between infrastructure scale and citizen empowerment.

## Know Your Rights

- **Account Aggregator consent is revocable.** Under the AA framework, you can withdraw consent at any time through your Account Aggregator or Consent Manager app. If you've given consent and want it removed, request revocation immediately. The institution must stop pulling your data. Document the revocation request.
- **Free coaching does not require Aadhaar authentication (yet).** The PM's announcement referenced DPI as the delivery mechanism, but no formal guidelines have been issued. If a coaching platform demands Aadhaar-mandatory registration before the government issues clear rules, challenge it. "Voluntary" may not stay voluntary.
- **ONDC seller rights.** If you're a small seller facing onboarding barriers on ONDC, file a grievance with the ONDC participant platform. The DPIIT-backed network is supposed to reduce barriers, not create new ones. If GST registration is blocking your access, explore the simplified GST registration schemes for small businesses.
- **AI lending decisions are contestable.** If you're denied credit through a ULI-connected platform and suspect algorithmic bias (no formal credit history, rural, informal sector), you have the right to ask the lender for the reasons. RBI's fair lending guidelines apply regardless of whether the decision was made by a human or an AI model.
- **Data Protection Board complaints.** The DPDP Board remains unconstituted, but the legal right to data protection exists. Preserve records of any unauthorised data sharing or consent obtained without proper disclosure. When the board becomes functional, these records will be essential for filing complaints.
