## From the Memoirs of a Future Citizen

I remember the week of August 24, 2026, because that was the week the government of India decided that having a phone number was no longer a right. It was a biometric audition.

On August 21, the Department of Telecommunications notified the Telecommunications (User Identification) Rules, 2026. On August 24, the Digital Intelligence Platform went live. And overnight, the simple act of buying a prepaid SIM card — something my grandmother did at a paan shop in 2004 for ₹50 — required a live face scan, a fingerprint read, or an iris capture.

If you were an Aadhaar holder, you had no choice at all. Aadhaar e-KYC was mandatory. The government had spent fourteen years insisting that Aadhaar was voluntary, and then, in a single notification, made it the *only* way to get a phone connection. This was like a restaurant saying, "You don't have to order the set menu — but we've burned the à la carte menu, and the chef has gone home."

But the truly magnificent detail — the one that future historians will study with the same bewilderment that we study medieval bloodletting — was that you now needed a biometric scan to *surrender* your own SIM card. You couldn't even give your phone number back to the government without showing them your face. This was no longer identity verification. This was identity extraction as a condition of existence.

---

## The Digital Intelligence Platform: Intelligence Without the Intelligence

The Digital Intelligence Platform, or DIP as the bureaucrats inevitably christened it, went operational on August 24. Its job was to distribute a "representative image" of every telecom subscriber to every telecom operator, daily, to enforce a cap of nine SIM connections per person.

Let me repeat that. The government built a system that sent your face to every telecom company in India, every single day, to make sure you hadn't bought a tenth SIM card.

This was the digital equivalent of posting your photograph on every lamp post in the country with a note saying, "This person is allowed to have nine phones. If you see them with a tenth, call the authorities."

The Internet Freedom Foundation, which had been sounding the alarm on biometric exclusion for years, pointed out the obvious: this regime would lock out the people who most needed to stay connected. Manual labourers whose fingerprints had been worn smooth by years of bricklaying and quarry work. Elderly citizens whose irises had clouded with age. Women in shared households who didn't have independent address proof. Transgender persons whose documents didn't match their lived identity. Migrant workers who moved every few months and couldn't maintain a stable address. Persons with disabilities who couldn't physically present themselves at a store for a live biometric capture.

These were not edge cases. In a country of 1.4 billion people, the "edge" is the size of several European nations.

But the DoT had thought of that. They had provided alternative verification methods: you could use your face, your fingerprint, or your iris. If none of those worked, presumably you were expected to communicate via carrier pigeon or telepathy.

## The Week of Celebration: Cake for Some, Exclusion for Others

While the SIM biometric rules were locking citizens out of connectivity, the rest of the government was in a festive mood. August 25 marked ten years of UPI, and the celebrations were lavish.

The Prime Minister posted on X — formerly Twitter, now presumably renamed to something more befitting of a sovereign digital infrastructure — calling UPI a "major turning point." The Finance Ministry released a statement noting that annual UPI transaction volume had risen from 1.78 crore in 2016-17 to over 24,162 crore in 2025-26. That was a 13,000-fold increase, a number so large it required its own comma.

UPI was being hailed globally. It had expanded to 11 countries. The IMF had acknowledged it as the world's largest real-time payment system. India was in talks with nations across Africa and Latin America to export the model. The Finance Minister was in Chicago pitching "Manufacture in India for the World" with Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, and ONDC as the crown jewels.

Meanwhile, Protean eGov Technologies — the company that runs the Aadhaar authentication ecosystem under a UIDAI mandate — released its FY26 results: ₹998 crore in revenue, ₹100.59 crore in profit after tax, and a shiny new ₹1,370 crore contract from UIDAI. The company declared a ₹10 dividend per share and boasted of being debt-free with ₹850 crore in cash reserves.

So to summarise the week: the government was celebrating that you could now pay for things instantly, using an identity system that had generated ₹100 crore in profit for its operator, while simultaneously requiring you to submit to a face scan just to *maintain* the phone number that made those payments possible. The digital public infrastructure was free at the point of use. The infrastructure's infrastructure — the identity layer, the biometric hardware, the authentication contracts — was very much not.

This was the Indian model: a consumer subsidy layered on top of a citizen tax, where the tax was paid in biometric data rather than rupees, and the subsidy was the privilege of being included in a system you never consented to join.

## The Exclusion Arithmetic

The IFF's analysis laid out the exclusion math with surgical precision. Your mobile number in India was no longer just a phone number. It was the key to your bank account, your UPI payments, your Aadhaar OTP, your DigiLocker, your ration e-KYC, and your welfare payments. When that number could only be issued, kept, or transferred on a successful biometric scan, a failed fingerprint stopped being an inconvenience and became a lockout from public life.

The government had effectively created a biometric gatekeeper for citizenship itself. Not the legal status of citizenship — nobody was revoking anyone's passport. But the *practical* substance of citizenship: the ability to communicate, to transact, to access welfare, to prove you exist in a database that the state recognised.

And this was done under a legal framework — the Supreme Court's Puttaswamy judgment — that explicitly said privacy is a fundamental right and that any intrusion must be proportionate, necessary, and subject to legal safeguards.

The DoT's new rules were none of those things. They were disproportionate — a face scan for a SIM is not a proportionate response to the problem of fake SIM cards, any more than a body cavity search is a proportionate response to jaywalking. They were not necessary — existing document-based verification had worked for two decades. And the safeguards were non-existent: there was no privacy impact assessment, no data retention policy, no explanation of what happened to your biometric data after verification, and no independent oversight of the Digital Intelligence Platform.

## Exporting the Model: Buyer Beware

The same week, the government was telling the world that this was the model to emulate. Countries across Asia, Africa, and Latin America were exploring India's DPI stack. Reports called it a "global blueprint for inclusive growth." The rhetoric was about accessibility, affordability, and interoperability.

Nobody was mentioning the fine print. Nobody was saying: "By the way, this model requires mandatory biometric verification for basic connectivity. It generates ₹100 crore in annual profit for the identity infrastructure operator. It has been challenged as unconstitutional by civil society organisations. The data protection regulator exists on paper but not in practice. And the 'voluntary' identity system is now compulsory for phone connections."

India was selling the car again. Still no brakes.

## The Consent Theatre's Latest Production: Biometric Surrender

There was something almost theatrical about the timing. In a single week, India:

1. Made biometric verification mandatory to get, keep, replace, change, or *surrender* a SIM card
2. Operationalised a system that distributed citizens' facial images to all telecom operators daily
3. Celebrated a decade of "free" digital payments
4. Watched the Aadhaar vendor post record profits
5. Pitched this entire ecosystem to the Global South as a model for "inclusive growth"

Each of these stories, in isolation, was a policy decision. Together, they formed a narrative: digital public infrastructure in India was not built for citizens. It was built *on* citizens. Their data, their biometrics, their faces, their fingerprints — these were the raw materials. The infrastructure was the product. The citizens were the mine.

The consent theater had reached its final act. It was no longer about getting citizens to click "I agree." It was about making agreement structurally impossible to refuse. You could not opt out of Aadhaar e-KYC for your SIM because there was no alternative. You could not refuse to have your face in the Digital Intelligence Platform because the platform was mandatory. You could not choose not to participate in India's digital public infrastructure because the infrastructure had become the only way to access basic services.

This was not voluntary. This was not choice. This was a door that appeared to be open but had been bricked shut from the inside, with a sign on it that read: "Feel free to leave at any time."

## What Actually Happened This Week

**1. DoT makes biometric verification mandatory for SIM cards.** The Telecommunications (User Identification) Rules, 2026 (G.S.R. 750(E)), notified on August 21, require live biometric verification — face, fingerprint, or iris — for obtaining, replacing, changing details of, or surrendering a SIM card. Aadhaar holders must use Aadhaar e-KYC with no alternative.

**2. Digital Intelligence Platform goes live.** Operationalised on August 24 via DoT Circular No. 3/User Identification/2026, the DIP distributes a "representative image" of subscribers to all telecom operators daily to enforce a cap of nine connections per person.

**3. Internet Freedom Foundation raises exclusion and constitutional concerns.** IFF published a detailed analysis arguing the regime is unconstitutional under the Puttaswamy privacy judgment, will not meaningfully reduce fraud, and will lock out manual labourers, the elderly, women in shared households, transgender persons, persons with disabilities, and migrants from basic connectivity.

**4. UPI completes 10 years.** August 25 marked UPI's tenth anniversary. The Finance Ministry reported annual transaction volume rose from 1.78 crore (FY 2016-17) to over 24,162 crore (FY 2025-26) — a 13,000-fold increase. Transaction value rose from ₹7,000 crore to ₹314 lakh crore. PM Modi called it a "major turning point" in India's digital payments journey.

**5. Protean eGov posts record profit on Aadhaar contracts.** Protean eGov Technologies (formerly NSDL e-Gov), UIDAI's primary authentication infrastructure partner, reported FY26 revenue of ₹998 crore (19% YoY growth) and PAT of ₹100.59 crore. The company secured a new ₹1,370 crore mandate from UIDAI, declared a ₹10/share dividend, and maintained a debt-free balance sheet with ₹850 crore in cash reserves.

**6. India pushes DPI as global model.** An IANS report, widely syndicated, described India's DPI as a "global blueprint for inclusive growth," with countries across Asia, Africa, and Latin America exploring elements of India's digital architecture. UPI has expanded to 11 countries.

## Know Your Rights


- **You cannot be denied a SIM solely for biometric failure — but you can be.** The rules as written provide no fallback mechanism for citizens whose biometrics consistently fail (worn fingerprints, medical conditions, disability). If you are denied a SIM, document the denial, get it in writing, and file a complaint with the DoT's Grievance Appellate Committee. Challenge the absence of an alternative verification pathway.

- **Your facial image in the DIP is your data.** The rules do not clearly specify retention periods, deletion protocols, or secondary use restrictions for the biometric data collected and distributed via the Digital Intelligence Platform. File an RTI with the DoT asking: (a) what is the data retention policy for biometric data collected under these rules, (b) who has access to the DIP's subscriber image database, and (c) what safeguards prevent unauthorised secondary use.

- **Aadhaar e-KYC being mandatory for Aadhaar holders may be unconstitutional.** The Supreme Court in Puttaswamy (2017) and the Aadhaar Act itself prohibit making Aadhaar mandatory for services except where specifically permitted by law. The telecom rules' requirement that Aadhaar holders *must* use Aadhaar e-KYC (with no document-based alternative) may be challenged. Organisations like IFF and SFLC.in are likely to pursue legal challenges — support them.

- **Biometric exclusion is a digital divide issue.** If you or someone you know is affected — an elderly family member, a daily-wage worker, a person with a disability — document it. Photos, timestamps, written refusals from telecom operators. This evidence is critical for public interest litigation. Send it to the Internet Freedom Foundation (iff.org.in).

- **The DPI export story has your data in it.** When the government tells other countries to adopt India's DPI model, it is normalising the same biometric mandates, the same consent-free data collection, and the same exclusionary architecture globally. Write to your MP asking: what privacy guardrails accompany India's DPI export commitments? If we can't staff our own Data Protection Board, what exactly are we selling?

- **You have the right to know what happens to your face.** Under the DPDP Act (even if unenforced), you have the right to know what personal data is being processed, by whom, and for what purpose. The DPDP Board may be an empty room, but the right exists in statute. When the Board becomes functional, these questions will matter. Ask them now, in writing, so the paper trail exists.
