## My Two Proofs of Trust

In 2026, the state gave us two excellent reasons to believe in digital infrastructure. One was a number so large it could be seen from the satellite maps: 11 crore Farmer IDs, created five months ahead of the March 31, 2027 deadline. The other was a number so small it could fit in a child’s arithmetic book: one of eight eMudhra certificate keys was reportedly the integer **4**. The first number proved that farmers could be counted. The second proved that some of the machinery used to decide which websites to trust had been counting rather casually.[^1][^2]

By 2031, I had learned to keep my proofs in separate folders. *Identity* held my Farmer ID. *Trust* held the government portal’s security certificate. *Actual Life* was a paper notebook the portal did not accept.

I cultivated a leased plot. The field knew me by the mud on my ankles. The registry was more formal. It knew the titleholder. At the enrolment desk, a fictional clerk smiled with the practiced tenderness of someone whose screen had already declined to load.

“Are you a farmer?” she asked.

“I grow the crop.”

“Do you own the land?”

“No.”

“Then the system needs to verify your relationship with the person whose name it recognizes as having a relationship with the land.”

I admired the circularity. In the old days, a farmer needed rain. Now he needed the owner’s signature, a functioning portal, a matching record, and enough mobile battery to prove that the field existed while standing in a different office.

The government’s own announcement said States and Union Territories were taking steps to bring tenant farmers and actual cultivators into the registry, subject to applicable rules. That sentence was more useful than the celebratory total: it admitted that counting identities and recognizing every person who cultivates are different jobs. In Tamil Nadu, a farmers’ association separately said tenant farmers faced difficulty accessing welfare schemes, crop loans, and fertilisers through cooperative societies. That report was not proof that the new national Farmer ID had denied anyone a benefit. It was evidence that the underlying question—who gets recognized as the farmer?—was not an imaginary one.[^1][^3]

I asked the clerk whether the ID was voluntary.

“Of course,” she said. “You may choose not to register.”

“And if a service uses the registry?”

“You may choose not to use that service.”

“And if the service is meant to make access easier?”

“You may choose to enjoy the convenience of not having access.”

She entered “citizen has been informed of choice” into a box that was not optional.

The same year, another sort of identity was in trouble. Eight eMudhra intermediate certificate keys had been found weak enough to reconstruct; Mozilla said they could have enabled fraudulent HTTPS certificates and blocked them for Firefox users within hours. eMudhra said none of the affected intermediates had issued end-user certificates, and MeitY said the incident did not affect regulated Digital Signature Certificate and eSign services. Those are important limits. No confirmed fraudulent website certificate or eSign compromise was established in the reports I read.[^2][^4]

Still, one key was the number 4. I felt a quiet professional envy. It had a shorter route to recognition than I did. The browsers acted quickly; the whole point of a trust system is that a user should not have to inspect the certificate chain over breakfast. But outside browsers there are other devices, apps, and software libraries with their own trust lists. The public question was not only whether the key had been misused. It was whether every system that needed to stop trusting it would learn that in time—and whether the public would hear clearly what was affected, what was not, and why.[^2][^4]

That was our national service model in miniature. The farmer was asked to prove he belonged in the registry. The registry’s public count was treated as proof that the system belonged to farmers. The website’s certificate was trusted until the people maintaining trust lists said otherwise. Then we were told the response had worked, which it had—and also that the underlying failure deserved investigation, which it did.

At a hearing in 2031, I asked a fictional official: “What does 11 crore measure?”

“Farmer IDs created.”

“Does it measure tenant cultivators included?”

“That is under active consideration.”

“Does it measure incorrect matches, rejected applications, or appeals resolved?”

“Those are different indicators.”

“Then what does the headline measure?”

“Progress.”

I asked what “trusted” meant after the certificate story.

“It means the trusted list is current.”

“Who checks that?”

“The trust system.”

“Who checks the trust system?”

The official looked relieved. “That question is outside the scope of this service.”

The joke, if there is one, is not that digital infrastructure is useless. A well-run registry can reduce repeated paperwork; a well-run certificate system can help protect people from impostor websites. The joke is that systems claim success by counting what they can see, then ask citizens to absorb what they cannot. A number of IDs is not a count of people left out. A certificate warning prevented is not a public explanation. A “choice” is not meaningful if every alternative leads to another counter.

In the future, my land record and my browser both displayed a green tick. One had verified the owner. The other had verified the connection. I was still waiting for a field that could verify the person doing the work.

## Factual Summary of the Events Satirized

On October 24, the Agriculture Ministry announced that 11 crore Farmer IDs had been created by the night of October 23—five months before the March 31, 2027 deadline—and said more than six crore farmers had received benefits through ID-based delivery. The ministry also noted that States and Union Territories were taking steps to include tenant farmers and actual cultivators under applicable rules.[^1] Separately, The Hindu reported on October 20–21 that eight eMudhra intermediate certificate keys were exposed; browsers moved to block them, and MeitY said the impact on Indian sites and apps was limited and that regulated eSign and Digital Signature Certificate services were unaffected. eMudhra said the affected intermediates had not issued end-user certificates.[^2][^4] These sources do not establish a confirmed exploitation or a Farmer ID-caused benefit denial.

## Actionable Citizen Rights Advice

- Ask your State Agriculture Department or Farmer Registry authority for the eligibility rules and accepted proof for tenants, sharecroppers, and actual cultivators. Keep the application acknowledgement and copies of relevant land, lease, crop, loan, and insurance records.
- If an entry is pending, incorrect, or rejected, request the reason in writing and ask for the correction and review/appeal process. Do not assume a verbal rejection is the final decision.
- Seek state-wise figures through the department or RTI: registrations by cultivator/tenure category, pending and rejected applications, reasons, correction timelines, grievance outcomes, and the data-sharing and retention rules for the registry.
- Keep browsers and operating systems updated; do not bypass certificate warnings. If a government or banking site shows one, use its official support channel rather than entering credentials through an alternate link. The eMudhra reporting distinguished the affected public-web certificate hierarchy from regulated eSign/DSC services.
