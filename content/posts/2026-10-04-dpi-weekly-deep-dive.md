# DPI Weekly Deep Dive — Discovery Is Not Inclusion: India’s National Digital Tourism Stack | Week of September 27–October 4, 2026

## Executive Summary

On World Tourism Day, 27 September, the Ministry of Tourism launched the National Digital Tourism Stack (NDTS), a proposed shared digital infrastructure developed with the Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC) and the International Centre for DPI Innovation and Advancement (ICDIA). Its ambition is to make homestays, monuments, guides and local experiences discoverable across participating apps, rather than requiring each small provider to win visibility on a single closed platform. The plan names 23,000 tourism experiences and homestays across 28 states and eight Union Territories, a pilot from January 2027, wider scaling from July 2027, and a longer-term integrated travel pass.[^1][^2]

This is the most consequential new DPI announcement of the week—not because a new booking system is already live, but because it extends India’s open-network approach into a sector where discovery, trust, regulation and livelihoods are entangled. The government’s own launch document describes a roadmap, not a deployed service. Its October–December 2026 preparation phase has only just begun. The decisive questions remain open: what counts as a verified provider; who is responsible when a booking fails; what “payment assurance” means; which identity data is collected; and whether the network will produce completed, fairly priced bookings rather than just searchable listings.

The design test is therefore not “How many listings were uploaded?” It is whether a guide, homestay or community experience can publish once, reach customers through more than one app, keep control of its terms and receive timely payment—while travellers get accurate availability, usable support and a real remedy. The stack could widen the tourism market. It could also become a new registration layer, a data-collection surface or a government-branded front door that leaves platform power untouched. The gap between those outcomes will be made by its rules, not its launch-day language.

## The Story in Depth

### Context

Tourism is a large, unevenly distributed economic system. The Ministry’s World Tourism Day backgrounder reports 428.55 crore domestic tourist visits and 2.53 crore foreign tourist visits in 2025, alongside tourism’s estimated 5.22% contribution to GDP and 8.46 crore direct and indirect jobs in 2023–24.[^3] “Visits” are not unique people, and the national totals do not tell us how income is distributed between a large hotel chain, an online travel agency, a guide, a homestay household or a village craft cooperative. But they help explain why the ministry is treating digital discoverability as more than a website problem.

The supply side is fragmented by design as well as by geography. A traveller may book transport on one app, a hotel on another, a monument ticket on a third, and find a local guide through a social-media post or a personal referral. A household or small operator may have a good product but lack photographs, searchable inventory, digital payments, cancellation tools, language support and the time to maintain several platform listings. Being “online” is not a single state: an operator can be registered but not bookable, listed but unavailable, discoverable but unable to receive a reliable settlement.

India is not beginning from a blank sheet. The Ministry’s NIDHI+ portal is already a digital database and recognition/classification system; its September 26 backgrounder reports 63,740 accommodation units and 15,531 tourism service providers, among other categories, as of 23 September 2026.[^3][^4] The Ministry’s 2025–26 annual report also records earlier digital initiatives, including an e-marketplace concept for Incredible India Tourist Facilitators and Guides.[^5] ONDC’s current Travel & Experiences page reports more than 1,000 tourism experiences and 170-plus Archaeological Survey of India monuments discoverable across participating apps. These existing listings are useful context, but not a like-for-like baseline for the NDTS’s broader 23,000 target.[^6]

These figures are not directly interchangeable. NIDHI’s accommodation units are not the same denominator as the NDTS target of 9,000 homestays; ONDC’s existing experience inventory may overlap with the planned 14,000 experiences; and the NDTS document does not say whether its target is new, incremental or a total network count. The first implementation task should be a public baseline and a clear counting method, not a celebratory addition of unlike numbers.

### What Happened This Week

The launch was made at Bharat Mandapam on 27 September and was accompanied by the National Mission for Alternative Destinations, branded Dekho Apna Desh 2.0. The Ministry describes the NDTS as a shared layer for search and discovery, cataloguing, supplier credentials, ratings and reviews, grievance redressal, supplier payment assurance and service quality. The companion concept note adds verified digital identity for domestic and foreign travellers and UPI-based payment options for foreign visitors. It sets out three stages: preparation and development in October–December 2026; a pilot from January to June 2027; and nationwide scaling from July 2027 through December 2028.[^1][^2]

The target is 23,000 listings—14,000 experiences and 9,000 homestays—across 28 states and eight UTs. A longer-term vision is an integrated travel pass that could bring experiences, monuments and transport together. Those are plan elements, not features available now. The government has not published a tourism-specific API specification, operating policy, participant agreement, trust framework or service-level targets in the launch material. It has also not named the pilot locations or described how a provider gets verified and onboarded.

Dekho Apna Desh 2.0 supplies the policy purpose that a neutral booking protocol cannot provide by itself: shift demand toward tourism-ready lesser-known destinations, while avoiding places already under heavy visitor pressure. The Ministry says destination selection should involve state governments and consider distinct appeal, infrastructure and the capacity to absorb more visitors; it points to hill and mountain, coastal, heritage and cultural, and regional pilgrimage destinations. It also proposes bookable itineraries that combine accommodation, transport, meals and local experiences, with visitor data and feedback informing destination management.[^1][^7]

Five Ministry MoUs announced at the same event—with Airbnb, FlixBus, Glance, Eicher Group Foundation and MakeMyTrip—cover community skilling, destination promotion, responsible travel and related work. The release explicitly calls these collaborations non-commercial, non-financial, non-binding and non-exclusive.[^1] They should not be mistaken for proof that a platform has committed inventory, that the NDTS has a live commercial settlement arrangement, or that a provider can already take bookings through the stack.

### Why It Matters

The strongest case for the stack is that it targets a market-access problem rather than simply building another national consumer app. If a local operator can maintain one structured catalogue and be found through several compatible buyer apps, the operator can gain distribution without building a mobile product, buying advertising or maintaining separate integrations with each marketplace. Travellers could compare more than the most visible destinations and move from a monument ticket to a guide or local experience in one journey. This is the open-network promise: shared rules and interoperable connections, with competing interfaces and service providers.

But “open” is not synonymous with equal. Apps still choose what to show, how to rank it, what filters to offer and which providers receive prominence. A listing in a shared catalogue does not guarantee a booking, a fair price or a meaningful choice. To test whether the network changes market power, the public needs to see how ranking and sponsored placement work, whether sellers can reach customers across apps without discriminatory access fees, and what data each participant can reuse. Otherwise, interoperability may lower the cost of joining while leaving consumer attention and supplier dependence concentrated at the buyer-app layer.

A second significance is institutional. Tourism administration is split across the Union, states, UTs, local bodies, site custodians and private operators. The Ministry’s own model says states and UTs will help curate and validate offerings. That is a real governance choice: one national protocol can lower technical friction, but it cannot decide whether a beach, mountain trail or heritage neighbourhood has safe access, adequate water, licensed operators or spare carrying capacity. The digital network must point to accountable public authorities and respect local limits; it cannot turn a destination into an unlimited inventory feed.

Independent travel-industry analysis has framed the initiative around whether shared infrastructure can reduce the fragmentation of local supply, not around a launch ceremony.[^8] That is the right lens. NITI Aayog’s 2025 homestay study separately identifies policy fragmentation and recommends clearer, simpler state frameworks alongside capacity building.[^9] The stack can help a compliant homestay become visible; it cannot harmonise local licensing, resolve land-use questions, train a host or create a safe road to the property. Treating those constraints as “onboarding friction” would digitise the paperwork without solving the underlying problem.

## Technical Deep Dive

ONDC’s published model is an interoperable network, not a single consumer app or central catalogue. A buyer network participant (BAP) operates the customer-facing application and buyer support; a seller network participant (BPP) connects providers, digitises catalogues and disperses payments. A gateway can forward search requests across seller applications, rather than confining discovery to one platform. The buyer and seller systems then exchange standardised messages for discovery and order handling. ONDC’s protocol repository describes core operations such as `search`, `select`, `init`, `confirm`, `status`, `track`, `cancel`, `rating` and `support`, with responses returned through callbacks.[^10][^11][^12]

For tourism, that sequence is more demanding than a product checkout. A stay has dates, occupancy, taxes, house rules and cancellation terms. A guide or experience has a time slot, language, location, group-size limit and weather contingencies. A monument or transport leg may be governed by a public authority with its own ticketing and refund rules. The launch document’s “cataloguing” and “supplier credentials” must therefore map into structured fields that are specific enough for accurate comparison without forcing a village operator to become a data engineer. The protocol can carry a transaction; the domain model must make the service legible.

The network also needs a clear order-of-responsibility map. Under ONDC’s published role descriptions, the buyer app owns the customer interface and support; the seller-side participant connects the provider, maintains the catalogue and disperses payment; the provider delivers the stay or experience. A customer facing a no-show must not be bounced among them. ONDC’s current complaint guidance starts the process with the buyer app, which has up to 48 hours to respond and 30 days to resolve a complaint under the published rules. The tourism pilot should make that route visible to travellers. The NDTS launch plan names grievance redressal, but its materials do not yet specify tourism-specific complaint evidence, refund initiation or who bears liability when a service is not delivered.[^12]

Identity is another design boundary. The launch material proposes verified identities for travellers and credentials for suppliers, but does not identify an Aadhaar requirement or disclose what claims are stored, shared or retained. A sensible design would verify only what a particular booking needs, disclose the purpose and recipient, avoid exposing identity documents to every app in the discovery chain, and offer alternatives where a digital credential is unavailable. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act is the relevant national framework for digital personal data, but the ministry still needs to publish the system’s actual data flows, controllers, retention periods and user remedies; a reference to “verified identity” is not a privacy architecture.[^13]

The concept note discusses supplier payment assurance and UPI-based options for foreign visitors, but it does not specify acquiring banks, foreign-exchange conversion, chargebacks, settlement timing, liability for a failed service, or how a foreign visitor obtains access to the payment rail. Separately, ONDC’s current Payment Assurance Program page is marked “Not live yet”; it describes a proposed voluntary buyer-participant security deposit and badge, which may not be the mechanism intended for tourism. The distinction makes publication of the NDTS’s own payment design more urgent. UPI can be one interoperable payment option; it cannot by itself guarantee a homestay exists or a booking is honoured.[^2][^12]

The public technical baseline is promising but incomplete. ONDC lists technical resources, domain API specifications and integration materials, and its buyer-participant description sets out functions such as parsing searches, presenting catalogues, enabling checkout and confirming orders.[^11][^12] The NDTS now needs to publish its tourism-specific schema and versioning policy, test environment, registry rules, security requirements, conformance tests and migration process. Without these, “shared infrastructure” remains a policy promise that prospective participants cannot independently implement or audit.

## Government Perspective

The Ministry’s framing is explicit: use digital tools and AI to make tourism more accessible and personalised, while ensuring growth reaches local communities and destinations beyond the familiar circuit. The new stack is linked to a demand-redistribution mission rather than presented only as a way to increase bookings. Its plan requires Union–state collaboration and industry participation. The separate destination mission explicitly asks states and UTs to identify suitable places and assess their capacity; Skift reports that state and UT authorities will curate and validate stack listings, a role that should be set out in published participant rules.[^1][^2][^8]

There is a practical government precedent for a mixed model. NIDHI+ holds registrations and classifications, while the Incredible India platform already connects destination information with booking services. The Ministry’s homestay guidelines establish common national standards while allowing states to adapt them. NITI Aayog has argued for simpler, more consistent policy and better support for homestays.[^4][^5][^9] The stack should connect these systems rather than create a second credential that an operator must maintain in parallel. Recognition by the Ministry, a state licence, safety certification and an ONDC seller listing are different assertions; users should be able to see which authority stands behind each one.

The government’s credibility will depend on what it publishes before the January pilot: eligibility rules, validation authority, data-protection impact assessment, complaint responsibility, refund rules, participant fees and incentives, procurement or platform contracts, and a baseline inventory by state and category. It should also publish a technical and policy change log. “National” infrastructure without public documentation makes state participation and independent accountability harder, not easier.

## Citizen Impact

For travellers, the immediate possible gain is discovery: more regional guides, rural stays, cultural events and bookable transport may appear in apps they already use. A traveller should be able to see verified details, total price, availability, cancellation terms, accessibility information, local rules and a real contact for help. Foreign visitors could benefit from more familiar digital payment options if the promised UPI access works for their bank, phone and trip. None of those benefits should be described as delivered until a pilot actually processes and completes bookings.

For a host or guide, reach can be valuable—but only if listing is affordable, manageable and reversible. A provider must be able to correct an address, update a calendar, decline an unsuitable booking, understand commissions and settlement dates, respond to reviews and leave a participant app without losing its underlying business record. Digital inclusion means not making smartphone fluency, English-language copywriting or continuous connectivity a hidden licence to participate. Assisted onboarding, local-language help and non-digital support channels are core infrastructure, not optional outreach.

The main harms are familiar: fake or stale listings, misleading ratings, price discrimination, unsafe stays, data leakage, unreturned deposits and a complaint that disappears between companies. The Ministry’s own plan includes ratings, credentials and redressal, but these need rules against review manipulation and a named entity responsible for resolution. Traveller identity should not become a universal travel profile. Location and itinerary data can reveal sensitive patterns; collect the minimum needed to complete the booking and delete it when the operational and legal need ends.

There is also a destination-level citizen question. The alternative-destinations mission says visitor data should help track footfall against carrying capacity. That is potentially valuable if local administrations control the thresholds and the data is used to protect residents, water, ecosystems and heritage—not just to optimise tourist conversion. A “lesser-known” place is not empty supply. The community must have a say in whether, when and how it is promoted, and should have access to measurable benefits from visitor spending.

## Global Context

The closest comparison is not a national booking website but Europe’s effort to build shared data infrastructure for tourism. The European Commission’s Transition Pathway for Tourism calls for common data-sharing frameworks, interoperability and digital capability across a fragmented ecosystem of tourism SMEs and destination managers.[^14] The EU framing is broader and more federated: enable data to be shared for services and destination management. India’s proposal is more directly transactional because it is attached to ONDC’s buyer/seller network and names search, booking-related functions and payments. The distinction matters: data interoperability and commerce interoperability solve different problems and need different trust rules.

The global DPI comparison is similarly useful but should not be inflated. India has working open-network experience in commerce and mobility, and ONDC has an existing travel-and-experiences surface.[^6] Extending those standards into tourism could make a public protocol a reusable market layer rather than a government super-app. But tourism has inherently local obligations—safety, licensing, land, heritage protection, environmental carrying capacity and redress—whereas a generic order protocol mainly standardises messages and transactions. What India exports will be the governance model as much as the API.

## Looking Ahead

The first milestone is not the 23,000th listing. It is publication of the pilot’s rules and baseline during the October–December build phase. Before January 2027, the Ministry and ONDC should publish a tourism-domain API, the exact participant and grievance roles, supplier verification sources, privacy and identity design, fee/settlement terms, refund and cancellation flows, and pilot destinations chosen with state and community input.

A public dashboard should distinguish registered providers, searchable listings, available inventory, initiated bookings, completed stays or experiences, repeat transactions and resolved complaints. It should report geographic distribution and provider size, median time to settle, cancellation/refund rates, listing accuracy, language coverage, accessibility information and the share of bookings reaching community providers. For Dekho Apna Desh 2.0, publish destination-level visitor limits and how demand is redirected when capacity is reached. A count of listings is a rollout metric; these are outcome and accountability metrics.

The success condition is modest to state and hard to achieve: an operator can join without surrendering control of the business, a traveller can book with confidence across apps, and the destination can refuse growth it cannot sustain. If the NDTS does that, it will be a meaningful DPI layer. If it merely turns more tourism into structured data, then the country will have digitised the brochure—not opened the market.
