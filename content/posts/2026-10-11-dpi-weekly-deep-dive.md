# DPI Weekly Deep Dive — The Rate Card, the Missing Meeting: UPI MDR’s Four-Day Test | Week of October 4–11, 2026

## Executive Summary

UPI’s next policy test is no longer whether India can build a ubiquitous real-time payment rail. It is whether the institutions that govern that rail can change its price rules without leaving merchants, banks, payment firms and customers guessing. A 0.4% merchant discount rate (MDR) on specified person-to-merchant payments above ₹2,000 is scheduled to begin on October 15. Yet after industry requests for a delay, the UPI Steering Committee meeting expected to decide the issue on October 9 did not take place, according to Moneycontrol. As of this article’s publication on October 11, I could find no public official notice replacing the October 15 date.[^1][^2][^3]

Among the week’s competing DPI developments, this is the lead because a merchant-pricing rule for a national rail is scheduled within days, while a reported challenge to its date has not become an official decision.

The framework is narrower than the phrase “UPI charges” suggests: person-to-person transfers remain free; small-value merchant payments remain free; the government says about 96% of person-to-merchant transactions will be unaffected. But for eligible merchant accounts, the fee is real, the exemptions depend on merchant classification, and the consequences of a disputed or delayed decision are not theoretical. UPI processed 24.5 billion transactions worth ₹29.82 lakh crore in August, across 752 live banks, according to NPCI’s latest statistics page.[^4]

The central question is not simply “Who pays 0.4%?” It is whether the rollout will make the funding of UPI’s infrastructure more transparent and durable—or whether a late, poorly explained rate card will erode trust in the very feature that made the rail ubiquitous: a payment that works across apps without a visible price at the moment of use.

## The Story in Depth

### Context: from a zero-price rule to a protected-free tier

UPI was not born as an unpriced system. Its customer-facing simplicity was supported by a policy choice about who should pay for accepting digital payments. From January 2020, the law required zero MDR on RuPay debit-card and BHIM-UPI transactions. A Ministry of Finance statement in August 2026 described the subsequent amendment to the Payment and Settlement Systems Act as an enabling provision: Parliament changed the legal basis for determining which electronic payments must remain charge-free, and the UPI Steering Committee would decide whether and how merchant pricing should change.[^5][^6]

That is a structural change in the policy bargain. The earlier rule protected a broad set of notified electronic payments from charges. The new arrangement protects specified categories and leaves space for charges on others. A Gazette notification dated September 14 identifies UPI transactions up to ₹2,000 and RuPay debit-card transactions for zero charges; NPCI’s September 15 framework then sets the merchant rates and operating categories. The two instruments do different jobs: the government establishes the protected floor, while NPCI’s committee sets the detailed schedule.[^7][^1]

This is a rail at nationwide scale, not a niche banking feature. The Finance Ministry’s FY 2025–26 factsheet reports 24,161.69 crore UPI transactions worth ₹314.23 lakh crore, connecting 55.49 crore individuals, 6.5 crore merchants and 731 banks. NPCI’s later monthly data records 24.5 billion transactions in August 2026 alone. The systems that calculate and communicate the fee will therefore have to work across large banks and one-counter shops alike.[^8]


The old zero-MDR settlement did not mean there were no costs. Banks, payment-service providers, app operators and NPCI still had to maintain software, fraud controls, customer support, networks and settlement operations. The public-funding response included recurring incentive schemes. For FY 2024–25, the Cabinet approved a ₹1,500-crore scheme focused on low-value BHIM-UPI merchant payments: eligible small-merchant transactions up to ₹2,000 attracted a 0.15% incentive, while large-merchant transactions remained zero-MDR but did not receive that incentive.[^9] That history matters. India has used both a price rule and public money to make digital acceptance attractive; the 2026 debate is about reallocating that burden, not discovering that payments cost money.

### What happened this week: the calendar stayed clearer than the decision

At the start of the October 4–11 review window, the October 4 brief noted no new UPI policy announcement and treated October 15 as a previously announced change. The later daily briefs and Tuesday’s deep dive made the MDR dispute the week’s strongest candidate: unlike the week’s significant rural-water, security, AgriStack and digital-service updates, it put a nationwide merchant-cost rule four days from its scheduled start, with no public decision on whether that date would move. Tuesday’s deep dive reviewed the Supreme Court’s refusal to grant an interim stay, a procedural step that did not resolve the legal challenge. The Court sought responses from the Union, RBI and NPCI.[^10]

On October 8, news reports said NPCI was considering a postponement to January 1, 2027, after requests from merchant groups and payment-industry firms. Business Standard also reported that a higher turnover threshold for exemption—an annual turnover of up to ₹40 lakh—was under consideration. Those were reported proposals, not changes to the published rate card.[^11] On October 9, Moneycontrol reported that the Steering Committee meeting expected to decide on the delay did not take place and that no decision had been made. By October 11, the public record still offered no confirmed revised date. Reporting about a possible postponement is not a postponement.

The published framework remains specific. Standard eligible P2M transactions above ₹2,000 carry 0.4% MDR, capped at ₹300 for transactions of ₹75,000 or more. A ₹10,000 eligible transaction therefore implies ₹40 in MDR; a ₹75,000 transaction reaches the ₹300 cap. Certain essential or thin-margin categories, including railways, telecom, insurance, fuel and agricultural inputs, have a flat ₹5 fee above ₹2,000. Listed capital-market payments have a 0.02% rate subject to a ₹300 cap. Person-to-person payments remain free regardless of amount, and transactions of ₹2,000 or less remain free.[^1][^2]

There is also an exemption for small merchants in the P2PM category: merchants receiving up to ₹1 lakh a month through UPI QR codes continue to pay zero MDR on all their transactions. The NPCI FAQ says classification attaches to the merchant account, so a single payment above ₹2,000 does not by itself make an exempt small merchant liable. It describes migration to P2M when monthly inward UPI credits exceed ₹1 lakh for three consecutive months.[^1] This is a vital distinction: “small merchant” here is not a casual synonym for every business with low annual revenue. It is an operational category linked to payment receipts and account status.

That threshold can produce a different boundary from the one used in small-business policy. A shop’s UPI receipts are not the same as its total turnover or profit; the merchant may also take cash, cards or transfers through another account. Conversely, a seller with thin margins can cross the UPI-receipt threshold during a seasonal spike. The three-month transition rule reduces the chance that one unusual month triggers immediate reclassification, but it still makes the acquiring provider’s account records consequential. Merchants need advance notice of a category change, an explanation of the calculation period and a route to challenge a classification they believe is wrong.

### Why it matters: four per cent is not a measure of four per cent of the burden

The Finance Ministry says approximately 96% of P2M transactions will be unaffected. That is a transaction-count estimate, not a statement that only 4% of merchants will pay, or that only 4% of UPI’s value is exposed. Nor does it show how the eligible transactions are distributed across sectors, merchant sizes, QR versus online payments or profit margins. A small share of transactions can account for a much larger share of transaction value and fee collections. The government’s figure is relevant, but it cannot stand in for a public impact assessment.[^2]

This distinction changes how “limited” should be read. The ₹2,000 threshold protects a large volume of everyday purchases. It does not necessarily protect every merchant whose business is small, nor does the ₹300 cap mean the total fee on a high-value merchant payment is negligible. A ₹1 lakh eligible payment would generate ₹400 at the headline rate, but the cap would limit the MDR to ₹300. Conversely, an eligible merchant has choices: absorb the cost, renegotiate a service arrangement, shift transactions to another method or adjust prices. Those choices will vary by market power and margins. A neighbourhood shop and a national e-commerce platform do not have the same leverage over banks, payment aggregators or customers.

The number “4%” therefore hides three different distributions. One is the share of P2M transactions above the threshold; another is the share of transaction value those payments represent; the third is the share of merchants whose accounts fall into chargeable categories. They are not interchangeable. A count-based estimate can accurately describe how many payment events are exposed while saying little about the concentration of fee collections among high-volume merchants, or about the burden on a particular thin-margin sector. Publishing all three would let merchants, researchers and parliamentarians test the Government’s claim rather than repeat it.

The policy therefore has two distribution questions. First, who pays within the payment ecosystem? NPCI and the Finance Ministry say MDR is shared among participating banks, payment-service providers and UPI application providers; it is not a tax collected by government or NPCI. Second, who ultimately bears the economic cost? The formal rule places it on the merchant, and banks are to ensure it is not passed to customers as a UPI fee. But a merchant’s overall pricing decisions can still reflect operating costs. A prohibition on adding a separate charge at checkout is not the same thing as proof that no price will ever change.

The small-merchant fund is a potentially useful bridge: the government says an amount equal to 5% of MDR collections will support UPI acceptance and sustained usage among small merchants. But an announced fund is not yet a measured benefit. Its credibility will depend on published rules for governance, eligibility, disbursement, audit and outcomes—not just the percentage earmarked. The public should be able to see how much was collected, who received support, what infrastructure was added and whether acceptance improved in underserved areas.[^2]

The fund should be evaluated as a transfer mechanism, not a press-release promise. Its stated purposes include merchant onboarding and support for acceptance in underserved areas. If contributions are calculated from MDR collections, the fund’s scale will depend on the very fee framework that small merchants may find difficult to absorb. That creates an accountability test: publish the receipts and disbursements by geography and programme, and show that support reaches businesses that would otherwise face the highest barriers to digital acceptance. Without those records, there is no way to know whether the fee actually finances inclusion or merely labels a small slice of a larger revenue stream as inclusive.

### A policy deadline is also a governance test

The confusion is not merely a communications problem. A national payment system needs coordinated decisions across the government, NPCI, member banks, acquiring institutions, payment aggregators, app providers and merchant software. A rate card issued a month before its scheduled start may still require implementation work across thousands of systems. NPCI’s own FAQ says participants need time to update software engines and billing systems. If a potential change is raised six days before launch and the deciding committee’s meeting does not happen, a merchant cannot confidently plan whether to accept the fee, how to reconcile it or what to tell customers.

The legal challenge adds another layer of uncertainty. The Supreme Court did not grant the requested interim stop, so the published schedule was not suspended by that hearing. But the Court asked the government and payment authorities to explain the basis for the decision; there is no final judgment in that procedural step. “No stay” is not a merits ruling, just as an unconfirmed deferral is not a revised rule.[^10]

This is why the best near-term measure of success is not the number of transactions after October 15. It is whether the transition is legible: a dated implementation or deferment circular; a stable definition of merchant categories; accessible notices for merchants; a receipt that distinguishes a merchant fee from a customer charge; and an effective route to dispute misclassification or incorrect deductions. NPCI already describes a customer complaint path that moves from an app to the PSP bank, the customer’s bank and NPCI. Merchants need equally clear escalation for MDR classification and settlement disputes.[^12]

## Technical Deep Dive

UPI’s basic customer route is interoperable: a payer uses a UPI app, a payer PSP and remitter bank authenticate and send the payment through NPCI’s switch; the beneficiary bank receives the instruction and credits the merchant. NPCI’s participant model lists the UPI app, payer PSP, remitter bank, payee PSP and beneficiary bank. Static or dynamic QR codes identify the payee; the user authorises the payment, typically with a UPI PIN. The NPCI settlement document describes NPCI as the clearing and settlement agency, calculating members’ net debit and credit positions and settling them through member accounts maintained with RBI.[^13][^14]

MDR does not need to change the consumer’s authentication or the basic transfer of funds to introduce a merchant-side price. It adds a second decision and accounting path around the payment: is this P2P or P2M; what is the merchant account’s P2PM/P2M classification; what amount was paid; does the merchant’s sector carry a special rate; does the small-merchant exemption apply; and does the cap limit the result? The FAQ’s explanation that account classification governs the small-merchant exemption makes the merchant registry and acquiring-side records as important as the transaction amount. This is an operational inference from the published categories, not a claim that NPCI has disclosed every implementation detail.

For example, the same ₹3,000 payment may produce different merchant-side treatment depending on whether the recipient is a standard P2M account, an exempt P2PM merchant or a specially priced category. The payer still sends ₹3,000; the policy question is whether a merchant-side fee is calculated and how it is allocated after the transaction. That creates practical requirements for interoperable classification data, accurate settlement statements, reconciliation, fee reversals for refunds, and complaint handling. The public NPCI FAQ describes rates and categories; the implementation timetable and participant billing rules should be published in member circulars so that merchants can verify their own treatment.[^1][^15]

The RBI’s 2022 discussion paper on payment-system charges offers a useful diagnostic: payment-system providers need revenue to operate and invest in technology, but pricing also influences adoption, competition and who can accept digital payments. It deliberately presented options for public comment rather than declaring that one pricing model fits every payment rail.[^16] That is the right frame for the October decision. “Free” is not a cost model. “Sustainable” is not a sufficient public justification unless the costs, revenue path and effects on adoption are disclosed.

## Government Perspective

The government’s strongest argument is that UPI needs a continuing source of funds for security, resilience, customer support and expansion. A fee confined to selected larger merchant transactions is meant to leave consumers and most merchants protected while giving ecosystem participants a revenue stream. The 5% small-merchant fund is presented as a way to recycle part of the revenue into acceptance and inclusion. These are plausible policy objectives, not outcome evidence.[^2]

There is a public-finance choice beneath the legal change. The previous approach coupled zero MDR with targeted public incentives, including the FY 2024–25 scheme. The new approach gives more weight to ecosystem-side revenue, while retaining free categories in law and policy. RBI’s earlier debit-card MDR framework shows that merchant pricing can be differentiated by merchant size and acceptance channel, with explicit caps; it also warns that fee structure and transparency affect acceptance. A tiered design is not automatically fair, but it is a familiar regulatory tool.[^9][^17]

The government’s own August explanation emphasized that the amendment did not itself impose a fee; it enabled a later decision. That distinction should be preserved in public communication. Parliament changed the legal envelope, the Finance Ministry specified protected categories, and NPCI published operational rates. Each institution has a different responsibility, and each should explain its decisions in a form merchants can act on.[^5][^6][^7]

## Citizen Impact

For most people, the direct instruction is simple: the framework does not add a UPI fee to person-to-person transfers or payments up to ₹2,000, and the government says UPI apps cannot add a hidden platform fee. A merchant-side MDR is not a tax debited from the payer’s bank account. But consumers should still watch for a separate “UPI surcharge” at checkout: a merchant cannot turn the MDR into a new fee on the payer under the stated safeguards. If it appears, the customer should keep the receipt and complain through the relevant app, bank and NPCI escalation path.[^2][^12]

For merchants, the first practical check is not just the ticket size. It is their registered category and monthly inward UPI credits. A small vendor who is still classified in the exempt P2PM tier remains exempt even when a customer pays more than ₹2,000 in one transaction. Merchants crossing the threshold need written confirmation from their acquiring bank or payment provider of the effective date, applicable rate, cap, special-category status and settlement statement format. A policy that is easy for a large chain’s finance team to absorb may be opaque to a single-shop owner who sees only a net deposit.

Consumers should also resist two opposite claims: that “UPI is no longer free,” and that “nothing changes.” The payer-facing service remains free under the announced protections. The merchant-side economics change for a limited category. Whether that affects prices or acceptance is an empirical question to monitor, not a conclusion to assume in advance.

## Global Context

India is not choosing between a uniquely free system and a universal global norm of merchant charges. The Bank for International Settlements’ survey of fast-payment pricing breaks fees into three levels: system operator to participants; fees among payment providers; and providers to end users. It finds that countries use varied mixes, with many keeping individual transfers free or low-cost while merchant pricing differs by country and by regulatory model. This matters because a user may experience “free” even when banks or merchants fund the rail elsewhere.[^18]

The comparison also cautions against importing a single foreign price. India’s UPI has its own mix of NPCI operation, bank participation, third-party apps, public support and extremely broad QR acceptance. A rate that appears modest beside card MDRs can still change incentives for a merchant who built a business model around zero acceptance cost. A rate that protects small-value payments can preserve adoption, but only if the exemption follows merchants reliably and does not create a confusing cliff between categories. The useful global lesson is not that every system should charge merchants; it is that the price at each layer should be explicit, and its effect on participation should be measured.

## Looking Ahead

Three near-term outcomes are possible: the October 15 date holds with an implementation circular; NPCI formally defers it and publishes a new date and transition rules; or participants receive instructions that are not sufficiently public for merchants to plan around. The third is the worst outcome. A national rail should not rely on market rumours to communicate its prices.

Before or at rollout, NPCI and the Finance Ministry should publish a versioned notice answering six operational questions: the exact merchant-category mapping; how P2PM status is assigned and changed; how the standard, ₹5 and 0.02% rates interact with thresholds and caps; how refunds and reversals affect MDR; how merchants can audit a deduction; and where to escalate a disputed classification. A formal deferment should state whether rates change, which transactions are grandfathered and what date now governs.

Within the first month, NPCI should publish a baseline and a short dashboard showing fee collections by category and merchant tier, the share of eligible transactions and value, the 5% fund’s balance and disbursements, merchant onboarding, outages, failure rates, disputes and complaint resolution. The 96% estimate needs its denominator and method. The Court’s requested affidavits and later directions should also be tracked separately from NPCI’s operational decisions; a court notice is neither a policy endorsement nor a substitute for an implementation plan.

UPI’s first MDR test is therefore not whether a payment succeeds on a phone. It is whether participants can explain, calculate and contest the fee without pushing uncertainty onto the smallest merchant or the customer at the counter. A durable price model may be necessary. A durable public rail also needs a public account of who pays, what the payment buys and how people are protected when the system gets the category wrong.
