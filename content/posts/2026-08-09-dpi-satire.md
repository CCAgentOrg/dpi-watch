## From the Memoirs of a Future Citizen

It was the second week of August 2026. India's digital public infrastructure had found a new frontier: children's faces.

While the grown-ups were busy arguing about whether the Data Protection Board existed — it didn't, but more on that later — the state of Karnataka was quietly transforming 52,000 government schools into the world's largest facial recognition laboratory. 525,000 students. Children as young as six. Their faces scanned every morning by an AI system linked to the government's Student Achievement Tracking System.

Nobody asked the children. Nobody asked most of the parents. The system was, in the language of Indian DPI, "voluntary" — which is the word the government uses when it means "mandatory but we haven't printed the circular yet."

---

## The School That Learned to Scan

The Karnataka government called it "Nirantara" — a smart attendance system using AI-based face recognition. A mobile app would capture each child's face, convert it into an encrypted identification code, and verify attendance automatically. The official justification was efficiency: no more proxy attendance, no more fudged registers, real-time tracking of student participation.

The system would also, conveniently, verify recipients of the midday meal scheme. Because nothing says "we care about your nutrition" like scanning your face before you're allowed to eat.

A coalition of 31 organizations wrote to the Chief Minister urging the project's cancellation. Child rights groups called it unsafe and unnecessary. Educators argued that schools should be protected spaces, not surveillance sites. Activists raised the risk of deepfake exploitation — facial images of children stored in a government database, waiting for the next generation of AI to weaponize them.

The government responded with the standard DPI playbook: encryption would protect privacy, the system used "non-reversible identification codes," and the data was secure. This is the same government, incidentally, that couldn't prevent Aadhaar data leaks affecting millions of citizens. The same infrastructure that a Parliamentary committee would, in the very same week, flag for catastrophic biometric verification failures.

But sure. The children's faces are "encrypted."

Here's what nobody in the official presentations mentioned: when you normalize face scanning for six-year-olds, you are training a generation to accept surveillance as a condition of education. You are teaching them, before they can read, that the state has the right to scan their body to verify their existence. The lesson plan has changed. Along with maths and language, children now study *compliance*.

The midday meal verification was the cruelest detail. India's midday meal scheme exists because children in government schools come from families that cannot reliably feed them. It is a welfare programme for the most vulnerable. And Karnataka decided that the appropriate gateway for this programme was biometric surveillance. "Give us your face, and we'll give you food." That is not a welfare scheme. That is a data extraction programme with a rice subsidy.

## The PAC Report: Biometric Failure as a Feature

While Karnataka was scanning schoolchildren, the Public Accounts Committee of Parliament — a body chaired by a Congress leader, no less — was delivering its own assessment of India's biometric infrastructure. The report called for a comprehensive review of Aadhaar, flagging high failure rates in biometric verification.

The PAC's concern was specific and devastating: verification failures were leading to the **wrongful exclusion of beneficiaries from welfare programmes**. People were being denied food, fuel, and financial support because the machine couldn't read their fingerprints. Not because they didn't exist. Not because they weren't eligible. Because the biometric system failed.

This is not a technical glitch. This is a policy choice. When you make Aadhaar authentication the gatekeeper for welfare, and the authentication system has a high failure rate, you have designed a system that excludes people by design. The PAC called it a failure. A more honest description would be: structural violence by database.

The irony was exquisite. In the same week, Karnataka was adding facial recognition to a school attendance system — biometric infrastructure expansion — while Parliament was acknowledging that the existing biometric infrastructure didn't work. It was like watching someone install a new lock on a door while a committee was issuing a report that said locks don't work.

UIDAI's response was predictable: improvements were underway, the system was being upgraded, face authentication was being piloted. But the pattern was now clear to anyone paying attention. India's approach to biometric infrastructure was: deploy first, fix later, and if the fixing takes a decade, tell the people locked out that they should have waited.

## The Child Safety Plan: Protecting Children by Scanning Adults

But the week's most Kafkaesque development came from India's child safety apparatus.

A proposal circulating in government circles would require every adult in India to verify their age through Aadhaar-linked biometrics before accessing social media. The stated goal: protect children from harmful online content. The mechanism: turn every adult's Aadhaar into a gatekeeper for internet access.

Andhra Pradesh had gone furthest, with a Group of Ministers meeting in April 2026 concluding that the state would explore "age tokens integrated with DigiLocker" — a system where social media platforms authenticate your age bracket without receiving your Aadhaar number directly. The government's own Internet Freedom Foundation called blanket bans and mandatory age verification "a disproportionate response that can do more harm than good."

Let me translate this from policy-speak to human: to protect children from seeing bad things on Instagram, the government wants every adult in India to link their Aadhaar to social media platforms. This is the digital equivalent of burning down the village to protect it from the enemy.

The Digital Divide Daily could not have invented a better headline. Only 33.3% of Indian women have ever used the internet, compared to 57.1% of men, according to the National Family Health Survey. Aadhaar-based age verification doesn't just fail to protect children — it actively excludes the women, rural populations, and elderly citizens who already face barriers to digital access. You want to protect children online? Start by making the internet accessible and safe for everyone. Don't build a biometric gate that keeps the most vulnerable people out entirely.

The proposal also ignored a fundamental question: since when is Aadhaar an age verification tool? Aadhaar was designed as an identity proof — not a citizenship proof, not an age verification system. Its deployment as the universal solution to every regulatory problem — SIM cards, bank accounts, GST registration, school attendance, social media access — is not versatility. It's mission creep on a national scale.

## The Ghost Board That Isn't

And then there was the Data Protection Board of India. The body established under the DPDP Act to enforce data protection. The body that, as of August 2026, had no chairperson, no members, and no office.

A detailed analysis published on LiveLaw on August 1, 2026 — headlined "India's Data Protection Board: Established In Law, Absent In Fact" — documented the board's phantom existence. The government had sought nominations in May 2026. A further notification followed in June. But as of the publication date, no appointments had been made. The DPDP Rules, notified in November 2025, had given organisations until May 2027 for full compliance. The Board — the sole adjudicatory authority for data protection disputes — didn't exist.

Karnataka was scanning 525,000 children's faces into a system with no functional privacy regulator. The government was proposing Aadhaar-based age verification for social media with no board to adjudicate complaints about misuse. Consent Managers — the intermediaries meant to give citizens control over their data — couldn't register because the registration framework wouldn't open until November 2026, and there was no board to process the registrations anyway.

India had passed a data protection law. India had notified rules. India had even set up a phased compliance timeline with the aesthetic of good governance. What India had not done was build the institution that makes any of it enforceable.

The DPDP Act was like a speed limit sign on a road with no traffic police. You could technically be fined. But there was nobody to issue the ticket. And everyone — from schoolchildren to social media platforms to the government itself — knew it.

## What Actually Happened This Week

**1. Karnataka school surveillance expansion.** The state government deployed AI-based facial recognition systems across 52,000+ government schools to track student attendance and verify midday meal recipients. 31 child rights and civil society organisations protested, calling it unsafe, unnecessary, and a violation of children's right to privacy. Critics flagged deepfake exploitation risks from stored facial data.

**2. PAC calls for Aadhaar review.** The Public Accounts Committee of Parliament flagged high rates of Aadhaar biometric verification failure, noting that failures were causing wrongful exclusion of beneficiaries from welfare programmes. The committee called for a comprehensive review of the Aadhaar system, including data security and reliability of biometric authentication.

**3. India's child safety plan demands Aadhaar age verification.** Government proposals to "protect children online" would require all adults to verify their age via Aadhaar-linked biometrics before accessing social media. Andhra Pradesh explored "age tokens integrated with DigiLocker." The Internet Freedom Foundation called the approach disproportionate, noting that only 33.3% of Indian women have internet access, compared to 57.1% of men.

**4. Data Protection Board remains unconstituted.** Despite the DPDP Act being passed in August 2023 and rules notified in November 2025, the Data Protection Board of India has no appointed chairperson or members as of August 2026. Consent Manager registration — meant to give citizens control over their data — cannot open until November 2026. Organisations have until May 2027 for full compliance, but the enforcement body does not exist.

## Know Your Rights

- **Children's biometric data in schools**: Under the DPDP Act, data of persons under 18 ("children") requires verifiable parental consent. If your child's school is deploying face recognition without informed, written parental consent, you can file a complaint. The absence of a functional Data Protection Board does not extinguish your right to object — document everything and escalate to district education authorities.
- **Aadhaar authentication failures**: If you are denied a welfare benefit (PDS, LPG, PM-KISAN, scholarship) due to Aadhaar biometric failure, you have the right to alternative identification under Supreme Court orders (Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, 2018). Demand service through alternative ID. File a grievance with the respective department.
- **Aadhaar is not mandatory for SIM cards**: Despite the government "encouraging" Aadhaar for SIM activation, the Telegraph Act does not make Aadhaar mandatory for obtaining a mobile connection. You can use alternative KYC.
- **DPDP complaints**: The Data Protection Board may not have members, but the legal framework exists. When the board is constituted, you will have the right to file complaints about data misuse. Preserve evidence — screenshots, correspondence, records of biometric data collection without consent.
