# DPI Weekly Deep Dive — Agentic UPI: NPCI Builds the Rails for AI Agents That Pay | Week of August 31–September 6, 2026

## Executive Summary

The most consequential DPI development of the week was not an announcement but a leak with a deadline. On September 1, Reuters reported — citing three people familiar with the matter — that the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) is preparing a rollout of agentic payments on UPI, built around a new **Unified Agent Protocol (UAP)** likely to be unveiled at the Global Fintech Fest in Mumbai on September 8–11. If it ships as described, UPI would become the world's largest payment network with infrastructure-level support for AI agents that pay on a user's behalf — no PIN entry, no per-transaction approval, only pre-set rules.

The architecture is deliberately conservative. The protocol reportedly reuses two delegation primitives UPI already has: **UPI Circle**, which lets a primary account holder delegate payment authority to a verified secondary user — an AI agent would simply become one — and **Reserve Pay**, which lets customers block funds (currently capped by banks at ₹10,000 for up to 90 days) for multiple debits without re-authorising each one. Around these sit rule-based spending instructions, spending limits, audit trails, identity checks, and a liability framework whose details have not been disclosed. NPCI has not confirmed any of this.

The takeaway: in the same week UPI processed a record 24.51 billion transactions worth ₹29.82 lakh crore, its identity quietly shifted. For ten years, UPI has been a system where a *person* initiates and approves every payment. The Unified Agent Protocol would make UPI the first population-scale payment rail designed for software that pays on your behalf — and it will land in a regulatory environment where the consumer-protection rules were written for humans, not agents.

## The Story in Depth

### Context

UPI's scale is the reason this matters globally. In August 2026, it processed 24.51 billion transactions worth ₹29.82 lakh crore — its highest monthly volume ever, days after its tenth birthday. The IMF's 2025 working paper recognised UPI as the world's largest retail fast-payment system by transaction volume, accounting for roughly 49% of global real-time digital transactions. Three-quarters of that volume flows through two private apps — Walmart's PhonePe and Google Pay — and 86% of person-to-merchant transactions are below ₹500. It is precisely this high-frequency, low-value profile that makes UPI the natural substrate for agentic commerce: an AI agent that has to ask permission for every ₹200 grocery order is not an agent at all.

Meanwhile, the agentic-commerce standards war accelerated worldwide through 2025 and 2026. OpenAI and Stripe shipped the Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP) for conversational checkout. Google launched the Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) with more than 60 supporting organisations, built on cryptographically signed Intent, Cart and Payment Mandates — and in May 2026 contributed it, with Mastercard's Verifiable Intent, to the FIDO Alliance. Visa built the Trusted Agent Protocol (TAP) under its Intelligent Commerce programme. Mastercard's Agent Pay produced authenticated agentic transactions in Australia (January), at the India AI Impact Summit in New Delhi (February, in a controlled sandbox), Singapore with DBS and UOB (March), and a live first in Hong Kong. Anthropic published retail agent blueprints on September 2 — and its head of product told Reuters that early deployments show cart sizes up 30–35% and customers 60% more likely to complete a purchase. All of these are private consortia standards competing to become the plumbing of machine-initiated commerce.

India has been rehearsing this for a year. At GFF 2025, NPCI demonstrated a live agentic payment — Gemini as the agent, BigBasket as the merchant, Google Pay and Razorpay in the flow — and launched UPI for IoT devices. In October 2025, NPCI, Razorpay and OpenAI piloted agentic UPI payments inside ChatGPT. In February 2026, Razorpay and NPCI announced agentic UPI payments on Claude at the India AI Impact Summit, with Zomato, Swiggy and Zepto live in the pilot, using Reserve Pay and UPI Circle to keep a human consent layer at onboarding. In June 2026, Pine Labs launched P3P — India's first private agentic payment protocol built on UPI's Single Block Multiple Debit and One-Time Mandate frameworks, using the HTTP 402 standard — with digital-gold platform Gullak live. What has been missing is the national layer: a common, interoperable framework for registering, verifying and authorising agents on UPI itself. That is what the Unified Agent Protocol reportedly is.

### What Happened This Week

**The Reuters report (September 1).** Three sources told Reuters that NPCI is preparing agentic payments on UPI, with the Unified Agent Protocol likely to be unveiled at GFF 2026. The reported design: users give AI agents rule-based instructions on when and how much to pay; NPCI provides infrastructure for merchants to integrate directly; spending limits, audit trails and identity checks are built in; and NPCI plans a liability framework, details unspecified. Early use cases are expected to be low-value, frequent purchases — groceries first, with e-commerce platforms best placed to capture early demand. More sophisticated uses — agents ordering on sale triggers or investing at price thresholds — are expected later. The sources said the existing ₹10,000 / 90-day caps on Reserve Pay blocks may be revisited for agentic use. Inc42, the Economic Times, India Today, Firstpost and the Times of India all picked up the report; NPCI did not respond to Reuters' queries.

**The capability gap closed from the other side (September 2).** A day after the Reuters story, Anthropic published two blueprints for retail AI agents — a shopping agent that assembles carts and a merchant agent that monitors inventory — and pointedly stopped short of completing payments. As MediaNama noted, "the capability Anthropic's blueprint deliberately stops short of is the one India is building the rails for." The collision of foreign-hosted LLM agents with India's national payment rails is no longer hypothetical.

**The warm-up act (September 3–6).** The rest of the week read like a GFF preview. WhatsApp switched on bill payments over Bharat Connect for its ~500 million Indian users, landing 22,722 bill payments on day one as a Customer Operating Unit on NPCI Bharat BillPay's framework. NPCI showcased a live demonstration of agentic AI-based IoT payments with UPI. UPI AutoPay interoperability — letting users port recurring mandates between apps — is also reported to be on the GFF agenda. Each of these widens the surface on which agents can act: more billers, more mandate types, more devices.

### Why It Matters

**It makes India the first country with *national* agentic payment infrastructure.** Everywhere else, agentic commerce is being organised by private networks — card schemes and AI platforms negotiating bilateral trust. In India, the state-adjacent standard-setter is building the delegation layer into the public rail itself, the same playbook that made UPI, DigiLocker and DEPA into DPI: build the protocol as infrastructure, let the market compete above it. With UPI's 55-crore onboarded users and its 11-country international footprint, UAP is also a potential DPI export — the specification other fast-payment systems (Pix, PromptPay, PayNow) may adapt.

**It is the biggest consumer-protection test UPI has faced.** Every safeguard in UPI's design assumes a human at the moment of payment: the PIN is the consent artifact, the notification is the audit trail, the dispute is filed by the person who made the mistake. Agentic payments sever that link. If an agent mis-executes instructions you pre-authorised — buying the wrong thing, the wrong quantity, at the wrong price — who bears the loss: you, your bank, the PSP, or the company that built the agent? NPCI is reportedly working on a liability framework, but has published nothing. The Indian legal stack is not yet reconciled with the feature: the RBI's Digital Payments – E-mandate Framework, 2026 requires additional factor authentication at registration and on the first transaction, 24-hour pre-debit notifications, and caps recurring debits without AFA at ₹15,000 — rules that an agent shopping while you sleep collides with on every count. CERT-In's Digital Threat Report 2025-26 proposed mandating human-in-the-loop controls for agentic AI above defined financial thresholds, but no threshold has been set. And the DPDP Act's purpose-limitation and consent requirements apply squarely to the profiling that conversational agents perform as a by-product of talking — with Section 9's children's protections (tracking and behavioural monitoring barred) taking effect May 13, 2027, and no mechanism for an agent to know it is talking to a 15-year-old.

**It sharpens the concentration question.** PhonePe and Google Pay already process about three-fourths of UPI volumes, and NPCI's 30% market-share cap has been deferred yet again — to December 31, 2026, never once enforced. Agent rails could redistribute power (a protocol layer is app-agnostic) or entrench it (whoever controls the agent platforms controls discovery *and* payment). Which way it breaks depends on design choices that are still open.

## Technical Deep Dive

UPI's four-party architecture — payer PSP, payee PSP, remitter bank, beneficiary bank, all routed through NPCI's central switch — is unchanged by agentic payments. What changes is who occupies the payer role. The reported UAP design reuses two existing primitives rather than inventing new rails:

**UPI Circle (delegated payments).** Launched in 2024, UPI Circle lets a primary account holder add "secondary users" who can pay from the primary's account in two modes: *full delegation* (secondary pays freely within a cap) or *partial delegation* (secondary initiates, primary approves each payment). NPCI's procedural guidelines cap full delegation at ₹5,000 per transaction and ₹15,000 per month per secondary user, with a ₹5,000 ceiling during the first 24 hours after linking as an anti-fraud cooling-off, a maximum of five secondary users, and KYC-verified linking. The primary user retains full visibility of every delegated transaction and can revoke at any time. Under UAP, an AI agent is effectively onboarded as a verified secondary profile — inheriting the delegation, cap, and revocation semantics already battle-tested on family accounts.

**Reserve Pay (Single Block Multiple Debit).** Launched in 2025, Reserve Pay lets a customer authorise once — with full AFA — and block funds in their account (banks currently cap consumer blocks at ₹10,000 for up to 90 days) for the merchant to debit partially, per delivery or per service, without further authentication. NPCI has since extended it to all UPI-permitted funding sources, including RuPay credit lines. For agents, Reserve Pay provides the natural escrow: the agent can only spend what was pre-blocked, and the block expiry is a hard backstop.

**The UAP layer on top (as reported).** On top of these, NPCI is expected to provide: agent registration and verification infrastructure (so merchants and banks can distinguish a registered agent from an impostor); a rule-based mandate engine (caps by amount, frequency, merchant or category); identity checks on agent-initiated transactions; a full audit trail at the switch level; and merchant integration APIs so rule-setting happens in the user's own app, not the merchant's checkout. This mirrors — at national scale — what the private protocols do differently: Google's AP2 uses Verifiable Credentials (signed Intent, Cart and Payment Mandates) to create non-repudiable proof of what the user authorised; Mastercard's Agent Pay tokenises credentials so the agent never touches raw payment data; Visa's TAP authenticates agents through a trusted registry; Pine Labs' P3P anchors on UPI's own OTM/SBMD mandates over HTTP 402. Notably, P3P and UAP are complementary rather than competing — P3P is a private protocol that would run over the public delegation layer UAP formalises.

**The unresolved technical-regulatory seams.** Three frictions remain: (1) the e-mandate framework's AFA-on-first-transaction requirement versus delegation's design goal of removing per-transaction friction — presumably resolved at onboarding, but untested in dispute; (2) CERT-In's proposed human-in-the-loop thresholds have no defined number, so "above what value must a human approve?" remains open; (3) NPCI has said nothing about whether agents get separate handles and separate PINs — MediaNama founder Nikhil Pahwa has argued they must, by default, along with user-set caps by amount and frequency and a defined recourse mechanism, because "agentic commerce fails because there is lack of trust."

## Government Perspective

The government's posture is consistent with a decade of DPI doctrine: the state builds the protocol, regulates the risk, and leaves the market to build experiences. NPCI — a not-for-profit owned by banks and operating under RBI oversight — is doing exactly what it did with UPI in 2016 and UPI Lite, Reserve Pay and UPI Circle since: adding a capability to the shared rail so no single private platform can monopolise the next interface shift.

The regulatory scaffolding around it is assembling in the right order, if slowly. RBI's FREE-AI framework (August 2025) established the financial sector's AI principles — seven *sutras* from "trust is the foundation" to "accountability" — and 26 recommendations across six pillars, including board-approved AI policies, consumer protection, and independent assurance for regulated entities. The E-mandate Framework (April 2026) governs the recurring-debit consent layer UAP will interact with. MeitY and CERT-In have signalled that human-in-the-loop controls for agentic financial actions are on the agenda. What is missing is the specific, binding consumer-protection rulebook for agent-initiated payments: a liability allocation, a dispute window, a threshold regime. GFF 2026 is where the government gets to sequence that — unveiling the protocol before the protections would repeat the pattern Indian fintech watchers know too well: scale first, safeguards later.

## Citizen Impact

For the ordinary user, the promised upside is real: bills, groceries, subscriptions and recharges handled by an agent that knows your budget and your preferences, within hard caps you set — a genuine quality-of-life gain for the elderly, the disabled, and anyone whose month is a series of small, forgettable debits. The delegation-lineage design means the user keeps a kill switch: revoke the mandate, and the agent is locked out.

But the risks land on the same user. Merchant-side metrics — cart size up 30–35% — measure what the *retailer* gained, not whether the purchase was wise; an agent deployed by the shop is optimised for the shop, and manipulative upselling becomes an engineering problem rather than a sales tactic. Pre-authorised rules shift the burden of proof onto the consumer in disputes: "you set the rules" will be the first line of every bank's defence, and UPI's existing fraud adjudication already leans heavily on whether the customer "authorised" a debit. Prompt injection — a malicious merchant page hijacking the agent's instructions — and account-takeover-via-agent are fraud vectors India's banking-system grievance machinery is not yet equipped to attribute. And there is a privacy debt: an agent that negotiates on your behalf accumulates a fine-grained profile of your budget, preferences, rejections and household patterns — processing that DPDP consent notices were not drafted to capture. The minimum bar citizens should demand: separate agent identities (handles and credentials distinct from the user's), default-on caps with easy upward revision, real-time notifications for every agent debit, a defined no-questions reversal window for agent-initiated debits, and disclosure at checkout when the payer was an AI agent, not a person.

## Global Context

No other country is attempting agentic payments at population scale on a public retail rail. The United States and Europe are organising around card-network protocols (Agent Pay, TAP) and platform protocols (ACP, AP2) layered on credit rails used by a minority of transactions. Singapore's DBS and UOB processed the first live authenticated agent transactions in March 2026; Australia and Hong Kong followed through Mastercard. Brazil's Pix — the closest structural analogue to UPI — has signalled interest but shipped nothing comparable. India's UAP would be the first *default* rail where agentic payments are available to hundreds of millions of users on day one, and the first where the delegation layer is specified by the network itself rather than negotiated between private networks. If it works, it becomes the reference architecture every other fast-payment system copies — and a live test of whether public-protocol agentic commerce can out-execute the card networks and AI platforms.

## Looking Ahead

The Global Fintech Fest, September 8–11 in Mumbai, is the deadline this story is racing toward — agentic AI is one of its three technology pillars. Watch for five things: whether UAP is actually unveiled and in what form (NPCI has not confirmed); the liability framework's actual terms; whether the ₹10,000 / 90-day Reserve Pay caps are raised for agentic use; the fate of AutoPay interoperability, which determines whether mandates — the legal cousins of agent instructions — become portable; and any RBI or MeitY statement reconciling AFA requirements and human-in-the-loop thresholds with delegation. Beyond the fest, the meaningful milestones are the first bank live with agent onboarding, the first dispute over an agent-initiated debit, and the first enforcement action — because the real measure of this infrastructure will not be how many agents transact, but what happens the first time one gets it wrong.
