# DPI Weekly Deep Dive — The Consent Layer Opens Without Its Referee: DPDP Consent Managers and the Empty Data Protection Board | Week of November 9–15, 2026

## Executive Summary

On November 13, 2026 — exactly one year after the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified — three provisions of India's data-protection framework quietly came into force: Section 6(9) of the DPDP Act, which requires every consent manager to be registered with the Data Protection Board; Section 27(1)(d), which empowers the Board to inquire into breaches of registration conditions; and Rule 4 of the DPDP Rules, 2025, which prescribes the registration framework itself.[^1][^2] Consent managers are the citizen-facing plumbing of India's entire privacy regime: registered intermediaries through which an individual can give, manage, review and withdraw consent across every data fiduciary from a single, interoperable platform. This week marked the first moment the DPDP's phased implementation touched the market — and the first time an obligation in force routes to the regulator that must administer it.

There is only one problem, and it is not small: the Data Protection Board of India has no members. The Gazette notification of November 13, 2025 fixed the Board at four members plus a Chairperson;[^3] MeitY invited applications for all five posts on May 6, 2026, advertised them in the Employment News on June 6, and confirmed the advertisement in a Lok Sabha written answer on August 12 — yet as of this week, no legal tracker, compliance publisher or news report records a single appointment.[^1][^4][^5] A Kerala High Court hearing on October 7 produced an eight-week expectation that the Board be functional — a deadline that lands around December 2, 2026.[^3] The Supreme Court, meanwhile, will hear the Digital News Publishers Association's challenge to the Board's constitution and the Act's journalism provisions on November 17.[^6]

This is the week's most consequential DPI story because it exposes the design pattern now repeating across India's digital infrastructure build-out: obligations go live on statutory clocks while the institutions that make them real — a regulator here, an implementation date there — lag behind. The same week, NPCI still had not announced the UPI MDR implementation date it promised "immediately after Diwali," even as it notified an acceptance-infrastructure scheme funded from MDR revenues that cannot yet be collected.[^7][^8] India is becoming world-class at legislating digital public infrastructure and stubbornly slow at staffing it. With the full DPDP compliance cliff — notice-and-consent duties, breach reporting, penalties up to ₹250 crore per category — arriving on May 13, 2027,[^9] the empty Board is no longer a transitional curiosity. It is the critical-path item for every data right Indian citizens were promised.

## The Story in Depth

### Context: consent infrastructure as public infrastructure

The DPDP Act, 2023 was framed around a consent-first model: personal data may be processed only with the data principal's free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous consent, or under narrow "legitimate uses."[^10] The Act's drafters understood that making consent real for a billion-plus people cannot mean navigating a thousand privacy policies. Section 6(7) therefore creates an enabling framework: a data principal may give, manage, review or otherwise withdraw consent through a **consent manager** — defined as a person registered with the Board who acts as a single point of contact and provides an "accessible, transparent and interoperable platform."[^11] The idea traces to the Srikrishna Committee's 2017 report, which envisioned a trusted intermediary operating a consent "dashboard" between citizens and businesses,[^12] and matured through NITI Aayog's Data Empowerment and Protection Architecture (DEPA), which described consent managers as "data blind" — conduits for consent that never see or use the underlying personal data.[^13]

India has already field-tested this institutional form. The Reserve Bank's Account Aggregator framework, operational since September 2021, regulates consent for financial data through licensed NBFC-AAs handling machine-readable "consent artefacts"; Sahamati, the industry collective, reports exponential growth in consent volumes since.[^14][^15] The DPDP consent manager generalises the model beyond finance — health, education, telecom, e-commerce — under a different regulator. The DPDP Rules, 2025, notified November 13, 2025 in Gazette notification G.S.R. 846(E), set a deliberately high entry bar in the First Schedule: an Indian-incorporated company with at least ₹2 crore net worth, adequate business volume and earning prospects, key managerial personnel of proven integrity, independent certification of an interoperable platform, seven years of consent records, no subcontracting of key functions, and freedom from conflicts of interest with data fiduciaries.[^16] Using a consent manager remains voluntary for citizens; honouring one's outputs will not be optional for fiduciaries.[^17]

The commencement design was always three-phased.[^9] Phase one, November 13, 2025: definitions and the Board's establishment provisions (Sections 18–26, Rules 17–21). Phase two, November 13, 2026: the consent-manager regime (Section 6(9), Section 27(1)(d), Rule 4). Phase three, May 13, 2027: everything citizens would recognise as data protection — itemised notices in the scheduled languages, security safeguards, breach reporting within 72 hours to the Board, children's-data rules, data-principal rights, Significant Data Fiduciary duties, and the penalty schedule topping out at ₹250 crore per breach category.[^1][^9] Phase two is the hinge: it is the only phase that creates obligations routing *through* the Board before the Board's own enforcement powers commence, chosen so that the registration pipeline is warm before May 2027. That logic only works if the Board exists.

### What happened this week: the hinge turned, the door behind it was bare

**The registration regime went live on schedule.** November 13 made Rule 4 operative, one year after notification to the day — Section 6(9) requiring registration "in such manner and subject to such technical, operational, financial and other conditions as may be prescribed," and Section 27(1)(d) switching on the Board's power to police those conditions.[^2] For ordinary data fiduciaries, the date creates no new duty; it matters to entities that intend to become consent managers or to integrate with one.[^17] Vendors have spent months positioning for exactly this window — account aggregators, consent-technology platforms and identity companies are the obvious first applicants, though an RBI licence as an account aggregator is explicitly *not* a DPDP registration.[^15]

**But no one can approve an application.** The Board's paper trail makes the gap stark. Gazette notification G.S.R. 845(E) — a two-page instrument with one operative sentence, file number AA-11038/1/2025-CL&ES — fixed the Board at four members on November 13, 2025, read against Section 19(1) as four Members plus the Chairperson.[^3] MeitY's Personnel-I circular of May 6, 2026 (F. No. 2(1)/2026-Pers.I) invited applications for all five posts: applicants aged 55 or above, serving up to two years or until 65, with the Chairperson needing Additional Secretary-rank experience and Members Joint Secretary-rank experience plus five years in a relevant field, at least one Member a legal expert, at consolidated pay of ₹4.5 lakh and ₹4 lakh per month respectively.[^3] The Employment News advertisement followed June 6; a Lok Sabha written answer confirmed the process on August 12.[^4] Since then: LiveLaw reported on August 1 that the Board had neither Chairperson nor Members eight months into the rules; Sansa Legal described it as "pre-operational" on August 12; MirvoLegal confirmed the same on September 20; Data Adhikaar saw no appointment through mid-October; DPDP Guard's November 1 status check still found every seat empty.[^3] False reports of June appointments circulated and had to be corrected — itself evidence of how thin the official record is: four notifications, one circular, one advertisement, and nothing else.[^3]

**Courts are now setting the deadlines the executive missed.** In April 2026, a High Court declined writ relief on the assumption that a petitioner could take an "efficacious statutory remedy" before the Board — an institution that, on the public record, could not receive a representation, hold a hearing, or pass the "reasoned and speaking order" required of it within fifteen days.[^5] On October 7, 2026, the Kerala High Court, hearing a petition on the Board's non-constitution, signalled it expects the Board functional within eight weeks — roughly December 2, 2026.[^3] Whalesbook reported on October 30 that MeitY was moving to finalise appointments, naming no candidates.[^3] And on November 17, the Supreme Court takes up the DNPA petition — notice issued October 27 by a bench of CJI Surya Kant and Justices Bagchi and Mohana, with an interim-stay application pending — challenging not just Section 44(3)'s dilution of the RTI Act's personal-information exemption but Sections 18, 19 and 27 themselves: the executive-led appointment process and the absence of any judicial member on a body that wields ₹250-crore penalties.[^6][^18][^19] The constitution of the Board is now literally sub judice while its seats sit empty.

**The compliance economy priced the deadline anyway.** ETCFO reported this week that CFOs have spent six to nine months onboarding enterprise consent-management software and hardening security, with third-party supply-chain risk the hardest part and fines of ₹50–250 crore the motivator.[^20] Enterprises integrating with consent managers were told to treat November 2026 as an integration start date, not a decision date.[^21] Yet as of November 10, legal analysts could locate neither a published application form nor a live portal, and PPC Land reports no tracker has recorded a single application or registration.[^1][^22] One compliance analysis put it bluntly: applicants need independent certification against a framework only the Board can publish — and two analyses could not locate that framework anywhere.[^1]

**The parallel dates slipped too.** The MDR story ran on the same rails this week: the 0.4% UPI merchant charge, deferred on October 13 "until after Diwali," remained unannounced days past the festival, with NPCI telling The Hindu there was "no announcement on this at the moment" even as it notified the Vyapaar Saksham scheme on November 11 — funded by 5% of every participant's MDR share, commencing "from the date MDR is implemented."[^7][^8] Pine Labs and AvenuesAI are building revenue models on a notification that does not exist. A consent-manager regime awaiting its regulator; an MDR regime awaiting its date: November delivered two infrastructure launches that are complete in every respect except the one that makes them real.

### Why it matters

Regulators are not overhead on infrastructure; they are load-bearing. The Board is designed to receive breach reports within 72 hours, adjudicate citizen complaints, register and supervise consent managers, and impose penalties from May 13, 2027, with appeals to TDSAT.[^3] Every other DPI layer leans on that trust function: the payments rails pushing ₹31.53 lakh crore a month, the health stack writing state hospitals into ABDM, the agri-stack gating welfare on farmer IDs — all process personal data at population scale under a law whose referee has yet to be constituted. The breaches are not hypothetical: SBI disclosed 3,897 customers' data exposed and surfaced on the dark web in October, and Bank of Baroda's July breach preceded it, both under a regime whose detailed obligations are not yet enforceable.[^20][^23] A consent regime without a functioning Board is a promise with a signature and no counterparty.

The sequencing also has a quality cost. Europe's experience is instructive: roughly 40% of the €7.1 billion in GDPR fines announced over eight years has been annulled or is under challenge, much of it on procedural and institutional grounds — evidence that under-built regulators produce reversible enforcement.[^3] India's Board members will serve terms capped at two years, will start with no case law of their own, and will face their first complaints within months of May 2027. The Digital Personal Data Protection (Removal of Difficulties) Order, 2026 (October 5, S.O. 5458(E)) showed MeitY can move fast on text; staffing is the slower, harder, more consequential fix.[^24]

## Technical Deep Dive

The consent-manager architecture is the most interesting piece of public-interoperability design in the DPDP stack, and its readiness depends entirely on instruments the Board has yet to publish. At the core sits the **consent artefact**: a machine-readable record of a specific consent — data elements, purpose, fiduciary, granularity, collection and expiry timestamps — created at the citizen's direction, stored for seven years, and propagating withdrawals back to every fiduciary that received the consent.[^15][^16] The consent manager itself is designed to be **data-blind**: it routes consent artefacts and holds metadata, but cannot read the personal data flowing between principal and fiduciary — the same data-blindness NITI Aayog's DEPA paper specified, and the same separation of consent-collection from data-holding that NITI Aayog argued gives DEPA an edge over Open Banking, where no consent intermediary exists.[^13][^25]

Interoperability is the load-bearing requirement — and the current gap. The Act requires every consent manager to operate an "interoperable platform," and the obvious blueprint is the Account Aggregator ecosystem, where RBI standardised open APIs so any new AA can "plug in" to a common sharing system without bilateral integration projects.[^15][^25] But the DPDP Rules leave the specifics open: ORF's submission to MeitY noted the rules are silent on who may perform the required independent certification of interoperability, what standards apply, and how "adequate capacity" or "earning prospects" will be judged — all left to Board discretion.[^26] That produces a hard dependency chain: no Board members → no published certification framework → no independent certification → no registration → no consent managers. Each link waits on the one before, and the first link has waited a year. Meanwhile fiduciaries face a subtle trap noted by practitioners: they are not required to hire a consent manager, but from May 13, 2027 they must be able to *receive and honour* consent and withdrawal requests that arrive through one — which means consent architectures built as single-application silos need export-ready, append-only consent logs now.[^17][^27]

The regulatory perimeter also has an unresolved seam where the two Indian consent regimes overlap. An account aggregator is an RBI-regulated NBFC handling financial data under the AA Directions; a consent manager is a Board-registered entity handling consent across all sectors under the DPDP. Analysis of the overlap identifies three consent regimes now operating over the same customer — the RBI consent artefact, Section 6 consent, and the Rule 4 consent manager — with two regulators, one user screen, and no published reconciliation; the AA interoperability mandate of December 31, 2026 lands mid-gap.[^15][^28] Sahamati's own submission urged the frameworks be treated as complementary, with DPDP interoperability read as standardised API integration so any consent manager can technically reach any fiduciary.[^14] The Board that must publish that reading does not exist yet.

## Government Perspective

The government's position is that the sequencing is deliberate and the delay transitional. Businessline reported government sources this week assuring the Board will be in place "pretty soon," while conceding "the process of approval is not entirely in MeitY's control" — the appointments run through two search-cum-selection committees (Cabinet Secretary chairing the Chairperson's panel, MeitY Secretary the Members', each with the Legal Affairs Secretary and two experts) and culminate in Appointments Committee approval.[^4][^29] MeitY has consistently billed the Board as fully digital and paperless — filings, hearings and orders online — and the May circular's eligibility terms signal a senior, lean regulator rather than a large bureaucracy.[^3] On the merits, the Centre has told the Supreme Court that press freedom is constitutionally protected, that citizens' privacy must be safeguarded, and that it remains open to dialogue on the journalism provisions.[^19]

The record shows the executive understood the clock. Economic Times reported in April 2026 — five months after establishment — that even the selection committees had not been notified; the May circular and June advertisement followed within weeks of that report.[^5][^30] In January 2026, MeitY floated compressing the 18-month runway to 12 for significant data fiduciaries, then let the idea lapse without amendment — meaning the date the government once considered pulling forward now coincides with a registration duty its own regulator cannot administer.[^3] The October Removal of Difficulties Order fixed two drafting errors in the Act, demonstrating continued attention to the statute's mechanics.[^24] What the record does not show is an explanation for why a Board established November 13, 2025 entered its second year unstaffed while statutory milestones accrued on schedule around it.

## Citizen Impact

For the ordinary Indian, the honest summary of this week is: your data rights got closer, and no further. What arrives on May 13, 2027 — assuming no slippage — is substantial: standalone notices in all 22 scheduled languages, consent itemised by purpose with easy withdrawal, grievance channels with defined timelines, erasure rights subject to retention limits, breach notifications, and penalties up to ₹250 crore standing behind all of it. Consent managers, once registered, promise a single dashboard across every app that holds your data — the DEPA vision of citizens directing their own information rather than photocopying it into every counter.[^11][^13]

None of that machinery has a human operator today. A citizen with a grievance about misuse of personal data has, in law, an "efficacious statutory remedy" before the Board — and in fact, no one to hear it, as the High Court episode in April demonstrated.[^5] Trackers confirm no citizen helpline exists and complaints cannot yet be filed.[^22] The regime's defenders note data-protection duties are not yet enforceable anyway; the rebuttal is that breaches are not waiting for May 2027 — SBI's 3,897 customers' records were already on the dark web this October.[^20][^23] There is also a structural equity question the consent design must eventually answer: the platform model assumes digital literacy, English or Hindi comprehension, and solo ownership of a device — assumptions that fail for exactly the populations whose welfare data flows through DPI most heavily.[^31] And the DNPA case will shape what citizens may ever learn about themselves through the press: Section 44(3) rewrote the RTI Act's personal-information exemption, deleting the public-interest override, which is why 21 news publishers told the Court the law could shut down newsrooms.[^6][^18]

## Global Context

The consent manager remains India's most distinctive contribution to global data-protection design — with no GDPR equivalent. Europe's consent tooling is business-side: consent management platforms helping controllers comply, CNIL-published dashboard standards in France, enforcement against dark patterns. India's consent manager inverts the direction: a regulated intermediary that serves the *individual*, accountable to the data principal, with conflicts of interest with fiduciaries barred by rule.[^11][^16] The International Bar Association has called it "an Indian solution for managing consent," and NITI Aayog has argued the consent-intermediary model is DEPA's structural advantage over Open Banking, which separates no such function.[^13][^25] If it works, it is exportable DPI in the same sense UPI was.

The empty-regulator problem, by contrast, is a global pattern with a distinctive Indian variant. Indonesia's Government Regulation No. 33 of 2026 sets fines up to 2% of annual revenue from mid-January 2027 while the authority it depends on does not yet exist; Egypt implemented its 2020 law only after a five-year delay; Vietnam's decree went live January 1, 2026 with its 72-hour breach rule attached to existing machinery.[^3] India's version is unusual in its completeness-on-paper: the regulator exists in statute, has a head office region, a fixed size, a published pay scale and eligibility criteria, and even court-imposed expectations — everything except members. The GDPR lesson India should internalise is about institutional capacity, not penalty size: 40% of Europe's fines annulled or under challenge shows that enforcement built on under-constructed institutions does not survive contact with appeals.[^3]

## Looking Ahead

The next ninety days are the densest stretch in the DPDP calendar so far. **November 17**: the Supreme Court hears the DNPA petition, including the interim-stay application and the direct challenge to the Board's constitution — a ruling that could reshape the appointment process itself.[^6] **Around December 2**: the Kerala High Court's eight-week expectation for a functional Board falls due.[^3] **December 31**: account aggregators must be interoperable, the first live test of consent-infrastructure design at scale.[^28] Beyond that, watch four signals in order: appointment of the Chairperson and four Members; publication of the certification and interoperability framework the First Schedule requires; the first registration portal and applications; and the first registered consent manager. Each is a prerequisite for the next; none has begun. If appointments slip past December, fiduciaries should game out what a compressed approval cycle means for their own May 13, 2027 readiness — and policymakers should ask the question this week made unavoidable: a digital infrastructure stack that ships rules faster than it ships institutions is building on a foundation it has not yet poured.
