## I. The Week Instagram Became a Training Dataset — And Then Stopped Being One (Temporarily)

**The News (July 7–10, 2026):**

Meta launched **Muse Image**, an AI tool embedded directly into Instagram. Here's how it worked: anyone could type a public Instagram handle into an AI prompt, and Meta's model would scrape that person's photos to generate new AI images of their face. No notification. No consent. No way to know it happened. Opted in by default for every public adult account.

The feature lasted **three days**. On July 10, Meta pulled it after a firestorm of backlash from privacy advocates, SAG-AFTRA (which called it a threat to actors' likenesses), digital rights organisations, and ordinary Indian women who immediately recognised that a tool allowing strangers to generate images from their faces — in a country where **99% of nonconsensual sexual deepfake targets are women** — was not a feature. It was a weapon.

India's IT Secretary S. Krishnan announced the government would **review whether Muse Image complies with Indian laws**. The Economic Times called it a privacy concern. NDTV called it a win for users. Meta called it a rollback. Nobody called it what it was: a consent violation so blatant that even the company that built the consent-theatre industry couldn't defend it.

**The Translation:**

> "We at Meta believe in giving creators powerful new tools. Like the tool that lets anyone on the internet generate images of your face without telling you. Don't worry — you can opt out. In Settings. Under 'Sharing and Reuse.' Under 'Allow People to Use Your Content.' It's only three taps deep. We buried it there because we respect your privacy enough to make it difficult to find. The feature lasted 72 hours. We learned a lot. Mostly that we learned nothing."

**The Satire:**

There is a particular cruelty in India's digital consent architecture: it is always designed so that opting out requires more effort than opting in. Create an ABHA ID at a hospital? One OTP. Want to understand what records it links? Forty-seven pages of documentation. Instagram launches an AI tool that turns your public photos into training data? Enabled by default. Want to opt out? Navigate to Settings → Account → Privacy → Sharing and Reuse → Allow people to reuse your content → toggle off Posts, toggle off Reels → hope the setting actually works → hope Meta doesn't re-enable it in the next update.

Meta's Muse Image wasn't a product feature. It was a consent stress test. The company wanted to know: *can we make your photos into AI training material without asking, and will anyone notice?* The answer: yes, and everyone noticed within three days. That Meta pulled the feature is less a victory for privacy and more a calibration error. They now know the exact threshold of public tolerance. The next version will be slightly less overt. Slightly more buried. Slightly harder to opt out of. This is how the consent ratchet works — not in one dramatic overreach, but in a thousand tiny ones, each one normalising the previous.

India's IT ministry "reviewing" the tool is equally performative. Under the DPDP Act, there is no specific provision for AI-generated likenesses. The law was written in 2023, back when "deepfake" was still a science fiction term and AI image generation meant DALL-E producing six-fingered astronauts. The government will review, find no applicable provision, issue a statement about "responsible AI," and Meta will try again next quarter.

## II. Digital India Turns Eleven — And Nobody Asked the Birthday Boy How He Felt

**The News (July 1, 2026):**

Digital India turned eleven. Prime Minister Modi marked the occasion by highlighting the programme's "transformative impact" on governance and citizen empowerment. Meanwhile, The Indian Express published a major editorial — co-authored by TeamLease's Manish Sabharwal and University of Minnesota's Ravi Bapna — arguing that **India's next governance leap will come from integrating AI with DPI.** The piece celebrated India as "the world's largest natural data laboratory," where UPI's 23 billion monthly transactions, GST's 20 crore payments, FASTag's 4.5 billion transactions, and Aadhaar's 27 billion annual authentications create a data ecosystem that AI can mine for "enhanced state capacity."

The same week, PM Modi visited Indonesia, where President Prabowo Subianto announced that Indonesia would integrate UPI and adopt India's entire DPI blueprint — including ONDC-inspired open commerce (ION), digital identity, and public service delivery platforms — for export across ASEAN. India's DPI was no longer just Indian. It was a template. A franchise. An export-grade surveillance-and-governance stack sold as "digital public infrastructure."

**The Translation:**

> "Happy birthday, Digital India. You are eleven years old. In human years, you're old enough to be questioned about your life choices. Like why your 'consent architecture' has more holes than a colander. Or why your 'voluntary' Aadhaar ID is now required to register property, file taxes, get a SIM card, and presumably, breathe. But don't worry about that — the pointy-heads at The Indian Express say you're about to get supercharged with AI. Which is great. Because nothing says 'trust-based governance' quite like feeding 27 billion Aadhaar authentications into a machine learning model trained by the same people who said Aadhaar was optional."

**The Satire:**

The Indian Express editorial is a masterclass in the language of digital authoritarianism dressed as techno-optimism. Let us examine the phrase: *"India is the world's largest natural data laboratory."* A laboratory. For data. "Natural" data. This is not how citizens describe their transaction histories, their mobility patterns, their identity authentications. This is how researchers describe a resource to be mined. The editorial doesn't say "India's citizens have generated the world's largest behavioural dataset through their daily activities." It says India is a "data laboratory." The citizens are the lab rats. The DPI is the maze. AI is the scientist running the experiment.

The "enhanced state capacity" argument is equally revealing. The editorial notes that government spending has increased 53,000 times since 1947 — from ₹198 crore to — and suggests that AI+DPI can make this spending more efficient. This is almost certainly true. But efficiency is not the same as accountability. A more efficient surveillance system is still a surveillance system. A more targeted welfare exclusion algorithm still excludes people. The question isn't whether AI makes the state more capable. It's: *capable of what, and accountable to whom?*

The answer, in India's case, is: capable of whatever it wants, accountable to no one. The DPDP Act exempts the state from consent requirements. There is no independent oversight body for AI governance. The Data Protection Board is toothless. And the "Jan Vishwas" philosophy — trust-based governance — is a beautiful concept that assumes the government has earned the trust it demands.

Digital India at eleven is a child who has been told it's special so many times that it believes it. It has Aadhaar (identity), UPI (payments), DigiLocker (documents), FASTag (mobility), GSTN (commerce), and CoWIN (health). Now it wants AI (intelligence). The missing element? Consent. Always consent.

## III. Smile for the Camera — You're Not Leaving the Airport Without Being Identified

**The News (July 6, 2026):**

The Internet Freedom Foundation (IFF) issued urgent representations to the Ministry of Home Affairs, CISF, AAI, and DGCA demanding an immediate halt to plans to install **facial recognition cameras at six major Indian airports** — Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai. The plan, reported by The Hindu, involves linking approximately **1.5 lakh CCTV cameras** under CISF security cover to a proposed **"Data Fusion Centre"** in Delhi, which would further integrate with **NATGRID** — India's national intelligence database.

The CISF Director General stated the proposal was to enhance "real-time monitoring, suspect identification, and support to law enforcement." IFF flagged the plan as a breach of the *Puttaswamy* privacy standards — no statutory basis, no proportionality, no oversight, no data retention limits, and no accountability for the inevitable misidentifications that FRT produces. IFF also filed an RTI asking seven critical questions, including: who is the Data Fiduciary, how long will biometric data be stored, and which agencies will have access.

No answers have been provided.

**The Translation:**

> "Welcome to Indira Gandhi International Airport. Please proceed to the facial recognition checkpoint. No, not Digi Yatra — that one was voluntary. This is the new one. The mandatory one. The one linked to NATGRID. We can't tell you what NATGRID does with your face data, because nobody has told us either. Please smile. Or don't. The algorithm works either way. Well, it *sort of* works. There's a 3-7% error rate for people with darker skin, and higher for women wearing headscarves, and higher still for children, and essentially random for anyone wearing sunglasses. But we're sure it'll be fine. The Data Fusion Centre will figure it out. What is the Data Fusion Centre? Excellent question. Next passenger, please."

**The Satire:**

India's approach to facial recognition follows a pattern that should be familiar by now: deploy first, answer questions never.

The proposed airport FRT system is not a security upgrade. It is a surveillance infrastructure project. The distinction matters. A security measure is targeted, proportionate, and subject to oversight. A surveillance infrastructure is broad, indiscriminate, and permanent. Linking 1.5 lakh cameras to a centralised "Data Fusion Centre" — a phrase that sounds like it was coined by someone who read too many Tom Clancy novels — and then feeding that data into NATGRID is not security. It is the construction of a panopticon.

The CISF's own DG admitted the system would be probabilistic. FRT systems are stochastic. They will always produce errors. Some of those errors will flag innocent people as suspects. Some will miss actual suspects. The question isn't whether errors will happen — they will — but who bears the cost. The answer is always the same: the citizen who is detained at a checkpoint because an algorithm with a known racial bias decided they looked suspicious.

IFF's representations will likely be ignored. The government's response to privacy concerns has been consistent: if the *Puttaswamy* judgment says you need legality, necessity, and proportionality, the response is to rebrand the surveillance as "security," the necessity as "obvious," and the proportionality as "proportionate enough." The seven RTI questions will be answered with "information not available" or "under examination." The Data Fusion Centre will be built. The cameras will be installed. And Indian citizens will have one more piece of their biometric data harvested by a system they did not consent to, run by an algorithm they cannot audit, stored in a facility they cannot question.

This is Digital India at eleven: a birthday party where the guests didn't choose to attend, the cake is made of their data, and the host is taking attendance through facial recognition.

## What Citizens Can Actually Do

1. **Lock down your Instagram.** If your account is public, anyone with the right tool (and there will be more tools after Muse Image) can use your photos. Switch to private. Opt out of Meta's AI training in Settings → Privacy → Sharing and Reuse. Check back regularly — companies have a habit of resetting privacy preferences.

2. **Refuse Digi Yatra — and the airport FRT.** Digi Yatra is "voluntary." The new CISF FRT system has not been justified under any legal framework. You have the right to be processed manually. Exercise it. If enough people opt out, the system becomes impractical.

3. **File RTIs.** IFF has shown the template. Ask your airport authority: is FRT deployed? Who is the Data Fiduciary? How long is data stored? Which agencies access it? Silence is consent. Noise is resistance.

4. **Question the DPI export narrative.** When politicians say "Indonesia is adopting India's digital model," ask: which part? The payments? The identity system? The surveillance architecture? The consent theatre? India's DPI is being sold as a governance solution without disclosure of its known harms: exclusion, authentication failures, biometric errors, data breaches, and the absence of privacy safeguards.

5. **Demand an AI governance framework — not an AI integration strategy.** The Indian Express editorial calls for AI+DPI integration. What India actually needs is an AI governance law: mandatory algorithmic impact assessments, independent audits, citizen redress mechanisms, and a ban on real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces without judicial authorisation. Integration without governance is a faster train to the same destination.
