# DPI Weekly Deep Dive — eMudhra’s Weak CA Keys: A Failure of Trust Governance | Week of October 18–25, 2026

## Executive Summary

This week, a cryptographic failure at Indian digital-trust company eMudhra became a public-infrastructure story. CERT-In’s 21 October advisory describes eight intermediate certificate-authority (CA) keys—six for TLS website certificates and two for code signing—that were generated with so little randomness that their private values could be recovered from their public certificates. The affected keys were P-384 elliptic-curve keys with values below 256. The curve was not broken; a key-generation process failed. Someone holding a compromised TLS signing key could have issued a certificate for a domain they did not own, potentially impersonating that site if they could also intercept or redirect a user’s connection.[^1]

The incident was reported to browser root programs on 17 October, just before this review window. Its public consequences landed during the week: eMudhra revoked all eight intermediates, CERT-In published a technical advisory, MeitY was reported to have formed an investigation team, and eMudhra’s board announced its voluntary exit from global SSL/TLS certificate issuance. Mozilla and Google blocked the affected keys; Google scheduled a broader removal of the emSign ECC Root CA–G3 from Chrome’s root store for 27 October. The company says the compromised intermediates issued no customer certificates and its Indian eSign and Digital Signature Certificate services are unaffected. CERT-In says public disclosures do not establish confirmed misuse. These are meaningful limits—not proof that the key-generation failure was harmless or that every downstream trust store has been updated.[^2][^3][^4]

This is the week’s most consequential cross-cutting DPI story because it tests the layer that lets people distinguish a real service from an impostor. India’s domestic electronic-signature framework and the international browser-trust system are related cryptographically but governed through different institutional arrangements. The public record does not yet clearly explain how those arrangements met at this key-generation ceremony, what the inquiry will publish, or how many legitimate services need migration. The central lesson is sharper than “use stronger encryption”: cryptographic trust depends on the process that creates a key, the audits that check the *published* key, and the speed with which every client—not just a major browser—can stop trusting it. The week also brought farmer-facing digital roll-outs and large service-usage snapshots; they show DPI’s spread, but remain layer-specific. This incident ranks higher as a cross-cutting infrastructure failure because it tested how users distinguish genuine digital services from impostors.

## The Story in Depth

### Context: one company, two trust functions

A certificate authority is a trusted signer. When a browser connects to a website, the site presents a certificate binding its domain name to a public key. The browser checks a chain: a root certificate already trusted by the browser or operating system signs an intermediate CA certificate; that intermediate signs the website’s certificate. If the chain is valid, the name matches, the certificate is within its validity period and other checks pass, the browser accepts the site’s identity. This is how the Web Public Key Infrastructure (WebPKI) makes HTTPS work across separately operated networks and services.[^10]

India also has a statutory public-key infrastructure. Under the Information Technology Act, the Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA) licenses and regulates Certifying Authorities for Digital Signature Certificates; the Root Certifying Authority of India (RCAI) signs the public keys of licensed CAs. Online eSign is a separate, transaction-oriented service: under the CCA’s e-authentication guidelines, an applicant is authenticated, a one-time signing key and short-lived certificate are used for a particular signing flow, and the signing key is deleted after the transaction. That is not the same certificate chain a browser uses to decide whether a public website is genuine.[^8]

CERT-In’s incident notice explicitly distinguishes eMudhra’s CCA-regulated Indian DSC/eSign operations from its certificates trusted by international browsers and operating systems. It says the reported incident concerns the latter function, which it describes as international and subject to global trust rules and independent WebTrust audits. That distinction is essential: a weak public TLS key does not establish that Aadhaar-based eSign, Indian DSC issuance or a customer’s signed document was compromised. But the existence of separate products does not answer the governance question. India’s published CCA audit criteria include controls for key management and SSL certificates, refer to WebTrust and CA/Browser Forum standards, and describe an audit regime for CAs in India. The documents do not, by themselves, show whether that framework applied to the specific global TLS key ceremony at issue.[^1][^7]

### What happened this week: a latent failure became a trust-store event

According to eMudhra’s preliminary account in a public Mozilla incident thread, an approved deviation from its usual key-generation ceremony added a wrap/unwrap test. A flaw in the ceremony script created a second, weak key pair inside the hardware security module; similar labels led the operator to use that weak pair for a certificate-signing request, which was then signed by the root and sent to Sectigo for cross-signing. eMudhra says the eight intermediates were intended for data-centre deployment in the US and Europe; seven never entered production, while one did but issued no end-entity certificates. Its current incident statement says the root keys and other intermediates were unaffected and it found no evidence of infrastructure compromise or a data breach. These are the company’s preliminary claims, not an independent final forensic report.[^2][^5]

That “no customer certificates” assurance does not mean the weak keys had no operational use. In the same public thread, eMudhra said that after a CRL signed with the intended key had an invalid signature, the CRL was re-signed with a flawed key. A certificate revocation list is part of the system that tells relying software which certificates to stop trusting. This disclosure does not establish that a fraudulent customer certificate was issued, but it makes the final key-use inventory and the history of CRL publication essential parts of the root-cause report.[^5]

On 17 October 2026, a researcher reported the keys to browser root programs and supplied evidence of control. Google says its Chrome Root Program received the report at 18:05 UTC, corroborated the findings and blocked the public-key hashes of the affected intermediates in a Certificate Revocation List Set (CRLSet) update at 19:45 UTC. Mozilla says it received the report at 20:06 UTC and completed OneCRL distribution to Firefox users with standard settings by 22:59 UTC—less than three hours later. These are strikingly fast responses by the two browser vendors; neither required most users to install a browser update or restart it. eMudhra says it revoked the eight intermediate certificates the next day.[^3][^4][^5]

On 21 October, CERT-In published its advisory. It describes six affected TLS intermediates and two code-signing intermediates. The P-384 private-key values were smaller than 256, making recovery from the corresponding public keys almost immediate. CERT-In stresses that the failure was in key generation, not in the P-384 algorithm. On 22 October, eMudhra announced that its board had decided to withdraw voluntarily from the global SSL/TLS certificate business. It says existing G1 RSA roots will continue to serve customers during the transition, revocation and certificate-status services will remain active until certificates expire or are replaced, and affected customers will receive replacement certificates without charge. The company also committed to provide a root-cause report to browser programs.[^1][^6]

The response goes beyond blocking the eight intermediates because a browser cannot assume every client receives a revocation update. Mozilla removed the emSign ECC Root CA–G3 root from its NSS trust store; Google says removal from the Chrome Root Store is scheduled to reach devices relying on it on 27 October, Pacific time. A root removal is broader than a block on the eight compromised intermediates. It can also make *legitimate* certificates issued under that root fail validation. eMudhra says the root distrust affects one US customer ecosystem and is arranging migration. Google likewise warns site operators that certificates chaining to that root need replacement. Root removal reduces residual risk in clients that do not consume intermediate revocation data, but it creates a real continuity obligation for innocent subscribers.[^2][^3]

### Why it matters: the weak point was the ceremony, not the curve

For an elliptic-curve signature key, the private key is a secret number, often written as *d*; the public key is derived by multiplying the curve’s generator point *G* by *d*. On a P-384 curve, a correctly generated private scalar should be unpredictable across a very large space. If the published scalar is instead a small integer below 256, an observer can test that tiny set of candidates against the public key. The curve’s intended mathematical strength cannot rescue a key chosen from a search space an attacker can exhaust almost instantly. The incident therefore is not evidence that all P-384 cryptography, the TLS protocol or encrypted traffic is generally broken. It is evidence that key generation and the ceremony that handles its output are security-critical components in their own right.[^1][^13]

An intermediate CA key has more authority than a normal website key. A website certificate usually identifies a particular domain; a CA key can sign certificates for many domains within its permitted scope. A forged certificate does not automatically let its holder read past traffic or decrypt every connection. To impersonate a service, an attacker would also need a way to place themselves on, redirect or otherwise interfere with a victim’s connection, and the victim’s client would need to accept the forged chain. But where those conditions exist, a convincing certificate can defeat the browser’s identity check and enable interception or alteration of that connection. The two code-signing keys raised a different potential risk: an attacker might produce a signature certificate that appears to validate software. That possibility is not a report that malicious software was signed or distributed.[^1][^4]

The best public evidence on exploitation is bounded. Google says it found no TLS certificates chaining to the six affected TLS intermediates in Certificate Transparency (CT) logs before blocking them. Because Chrome requires CT for publicly trusted certificates, Google says no certificate from those intermediates could have been used against CT-enforcing clients on the evidence it reviewed. CERT-In says the public disclosures reviewed do not establish confirmed malicious use. eMudhra says none of the affected intermediates issued an end-entity certificate. Together these statements weigh against known public-WebPKI abuse; they do not prove what every private or non-CT client saw, and they do not substitute for the final investigation report. CT is a visibility mechanism, not a magic revocation switch: public logs make issuance observable, while a relying client still has to reject a certificate or its issuer.[^1][^2][^3][^11]

The incident also exposes a common audit trap: verifying that a hardware security module is certified is not the same as verifying that the exact key and certificate published to browsers are the ones the ceremony intended to create. eMudhra says certified hardware security modules were used and attributes the weak keys to a wrap/unwrap step in the ceremony; it also says the precise root cause is not yet confirmed. The company reports that the certificates passed two independent audit cycles before the weakness was found. The important question for auditors is therefore not just whether an HSM was present or a ceremony checklist was signed. It is whether the key that left the ceremony, the public key embedded in each certificate and the version recorded in the CA repository were cryptographically reconciled—and whether that reconciliation was independently repeatable.[^2]

### Technical Deep Dive: three controls, three different jobs

A trust chain is checked locally by each browser, operating system, application runtime or bundled CA store. A TLS website sends a leaf certificate; the client builds a path through any required intermediate certificates to one of its own trust anchors. The X.509 path-validation rules specify checks such as signature validity, constraints and policy; the trust anchor is an input supplied by the client’s trust configuration. A root that is trusted by one product may be absent or distrusted by another. That is why “the browser is patched” and “every device or program is protected” are not equivalent statements.[^10]

The emergency controls in this case worked at two levels. Google’s CRLSet and Mozilla’s OneCRL distributed blocks for the affected intermediate keys, using the certificate’s Subject Public Key Info (SPKI) hash rather than relying only on a particular certificate serial number. This quickly blocks chains containing those keys in clients that receive the update. Root-store removal is a wider trust decision intended to protect clients that do not learn about a bad intermediate through such a dynamic revocation list. Both improve protection; the wider removal also means valid certificates under the affected root can stop working. For software outside the browser-update path—server runtimes, containers, embedded equipment and applications with private or bundled trust stores—the relevant vendor must incorporate the block or remove the root. Cloudflare’s response documents this operational breadth in its own platform and client fleet.[^3][^4][^14]

CT addresses another part of the problem: public logging of publicly trusted TLS certificates gives domain owners and monitors a way to detect unexpected issuance. RFC 9162 describes the current CT v2 protocol; X.509 path validation is specified in RFC 5280. Neither standard guarantees that every client checks a current revocation feed, and CT does not prevent a CA from creating a certificate in the first place. The standards work best as layers: safe key generation limits who can issue; CT makes issuance visible; client-side revocation and root-store decisions stop trust; software maintenance carries that decision to installed systems.[^10][^11]

For key generation, the useful control is not a slogan about “more entropy.” NIST’s random-bit-generation recommendations describe the mechanisms and entropy sources used to generate cryptographic random values. The CA/Browser Forum’s public-TLS requirements call for a documented key-generation script, independent witnessing or recording, an auditor’s report, activity logs and controls that show keys were generated and protected in accordance with the CA’s stated process. Those requirements make the exact audit question measurable: was the public key used in the subordinate certificate independently checked against the intended key-generation record, and were alternative or intermediate values excluded before publication? The published eMudhra account says the defect was in a transformation step, so a check that only confirmed the use of certified equipment would have been insufficient.[^9][^12][^13]

### Government and regulatory perspective: an overlap that needs a public answer

CERT-In’s advisory gives the most careful official scope statement: it separates domestic CCA-regulated DSC and eSign services from eMudhra’s international browser/operating-system certificates, says the former are not impacted, and records the company’s account of the weak-key creation step. Separately, MeitY was reported to have formed a special team to investigate; *The Hindu BusinessLine* reported that it included the CCA, CERT-In, UIDAI and NPCI. The ministry’s assessment, reported in the press, was that impact on Indian websites and applications was very limited and brief. No public investigation order, terms of reference, affected-site count or final report was located in the material reviewed for this edition; the team’s composition and the impact estimate should therefore be attributed to reporting, not presented as independently verified findings.[^1][^15]

The CCA’s 2025 Audit Criteria document complicates any simple “domestic versus foreign” explanation. It describes the CCA’s licensing and supervisory role, sets out key-management, subordinate-CA and SSL-certificate controls, and says its criteria draw on WebTrust and CA/Browser Forum requirements. It also describes annual CA audits. This shows that Indian regulation has tools relevant to certificate security; it does not establish that the exact global emSign hierarchy or April 2025 ceremony fell within the scope of a particular CCA audit. CERT-In’s description of the incident as an international browser-trust function is a scope statement, not a public legal opinion resolving that overlap. The inquiry should say plainly which entity operated each key, which audit obligations applied, what evidence auditors examined and which regulator received incident notice.[^1][^7]

There is an institutional asymmetry. India can license domestic CAs, require audits and coordinate incident response. But browser root programs and device vendors decide which roots their own products trust, and software teams decide how quickly their local trust bundles are changed. Those programs set published inclusion, incident-response and removal policies, and can act rapidly—as the Chrome and Firefox timelines demonstrate—while a CCA licence cannot by itself force an old embedded client in another jurisdiction to update. Conversely, an international browser’s root removal may interrupt legitimate services without supplying affected users a direct route to challenge the decision or recover losses. The policy question is not whether India should copy a foreign root store; it is how national regulators, global root programs, issuers and customers will share evidence and responsibility when the same certificate hierarchy spans all four.[^16]

### Citizen Impact: limited confirmed harm, real service-continuity stakes

For most people, the first practical effect may be a warning or failed connection if a legitimate service still presents a certificate chaining to emSign ECC Root CA–G3 after a client removes that root. A warning is not a reason to click through, accept a certificate manually or install a new root from an unsolicited message. Site operators must migrate to a currently trusted hierarchy; browser and operating-system vendors must distribute their updates; the operator must test the service on mobile apps, older devices and software that carries its own trust store. The government’s description of the India impact as limited is reassuring, but a public list of migrated services and a dated confirmation would be more useful than an adjective.[^3][^6][^15]

There is no published evidence in the sources reviewed that Aadhaar itself, CCA-licensed eSign, Indian Digital Signature Certificates or stored signed documents were compromised by these eight global TLS intermediates. CERT-In explicitly says India’s regulated eSign/DSC services were not affected; eMudhra makes the same claim about its separate services. People should not conflate an HTTPS website certificate with the cryptographic signature placed on a document. At the same time, “different systems” is not an audit substitute. The government should publish the basis for its assurance—what services and signing systems were scoped, whether key-generation controls were independently checked, and what conditions would trigger a reassessment.[^1][^2][^8]

The distributional burden is often invisible. Larger organisations can rotate certificates and maintain parallel providers. A small municipal portal, cooperative bank, hospital or school may depend on a vendor-managed endpoint and not know which root signed its chain, how to contact the issuer or what an outage means for a person trying to pay a bill or retrieve a record. A root-store action protects users only if affected site owners have advance notice, a replacement path and a way to verify completion. Free replacement certificates help, as eMudhra has promised, but DNS, hosting, change windows, testing and downstream-app updates still take people and time.

### Global Context: trust is interoperable, control is fragmented

This episode is a practical example of how global WebPKI differs from a national identity system. The public-key certificate format and path-building rules are standardised internationally, but trust is not decided by one world authority. Browser and operating-system programs publish different policies, distribute their own trust lists and revoke or remove trust on their own schedules. Mozilla’s incident report shows how a central blocklist can protect most Firefox users in hours; Google’s plan to remove a root a week later shows a separate cleanup decision with a compatibility cost. A server can work in one client and fail in another because their stores update at different times.[^3][^4][^9][^15]

The global response is a strength when root programs cooperate and a weak point when software is forgotten. Compared with a centrally administered eSign flow, web trust is more distributed: a user may have a vendor-supported browser on one device, an outdated enterprise application on another and an embedded device whose trust bundle is rarely updated. The standards travel; the remediation does not travel automatically. That is why the eMudhra episode is not simply a company security story. It is a demonstration that interoperability creates a shared dependency while leaving accountability and update power spread across organisations, products and borders.

## Looking Ahead

Four disclosures would turn this from a fast emergency response into a credible accountability record. First, the MeitY/CCA/CERT-In team should publish its scope, chronology and findings, including whether the flaw existed only in the described ceremony step, how many key-generation events and certificates were examined, and whether any other public-trust keys were affected. Personal or customer-sensitive details can be withheld; the method and aggregate outcomes should not be. Second, eMudhra should publish the root-cause report it promised, with independent validation of remediation and the mechanism that ensures no weak alternate key can be published again.[^1][^2][^6][^15]

Third, the regulator and root programs should clarify where their audit obligations meet. CCA criteria already point to key lifecycle, SSL controls, WebTrust and CA/Browser Forum requirements; the final report should identify which rules applied to this global hierarchy and how the two audit cycles failed to detect a weak key in the certificate actually distributed. Fourth, issuers and large service operators should publish a migration ledger: affected root and certificate chains, count of subscriber services, replacements completed, exceptions still open, and a safe help route. Root-store remediation should be tracked across browsers, Android, operating systems, popular language runtimes, container images and embedded products—not assumed from a Chrome or Firefox update alone.[^7][^12][^14]

The longer-term control is verifiable ceremony output. Every subordinate CA key should be checked, before publication, against independently recorded generation evidence; the certificate’s public key should be matched to that verified output; ceremony tooling should fail closed if an unexpected key appears; and a second independent process should test that no unpublished alternate keys can sign. Audits must sample outcomes, not only process documents. Certificate Transparency monitoring, clear incident-report deadlines and tested CA redundancy can reduce future harm, though redundancy cannot justify weaker controls at either provider. eMudhra’s planned withdrawal will remove one issuer from the global market, but it does not repair the governance model by itself.

The immediate facts are serious but bounded: weak subordinate keys existed; browser and issuer countermeasures were deployed; the company says no customer certificates were issued from the affected intermediates; the Indian government says domestic eSign and DSC services were not impacted; no confirmed malicious use has been established in the public disclosures reviewed. The unresolved question is whether audits and oversight can demonstrate that the *right key*—not merely a properly certified machine or a signed checklist—was the key the world was asked to trust. That is the standard every digital public infrastructure should meet.
