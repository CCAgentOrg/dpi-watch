## From the Memoirs of a Future Citizen

My name is Ravi. I am 34 years old. I have an Aadhaar number, a UPI ID, a DigiLocker, a CoWIN certificate, and now, thanks to Census 2027, an 11-digit Self-Enumeration ID. My grandfather had one identity: his face. My father had two: his face and a ration card. I have seven government-issued digital identities and zero privacy.

Let me tell you about the Great Census of 2027.

It began, as all great Indian government projects do, with the word *"voluntary."*

"The self-enumeration process is completely optional," said the Chief Minister of Tripura on July 17, 2026, while simultaneously urging every citizen to participate. This is the great Indian paradox of consent: the government says something is optional while deploying every resource at its disposal to make it feel mandatory. District magistrates made appeals. Politicians posed with tablets. Aadhaar-linked mobile OTPs were required to log in. "Optional" but Aadhaar-gated. "Voluntary" but you couldn't complete it without an internet connection, a smartphone, and a biometric identity that was itself once called "voluntary."

You see, in India, "voluntary" is a technical term. It means: "You are not legally compelled, but if you don't, a government employee will show up at your door and do it for you using a mobile app connected to the Aadhaar database." The door-to-door enumerator wasn't a backup plan — it was a guarantee. The system was designed so that whether you self-enumerated or waited for the enumerator, your data ended up in the same digital silo. Choice was an illusion. The form was the same. The database was the same. Only the method of input varied.

But here's what made Census 2027 truly special: it was India's first fully digital census. No paper. No pen. Just you, your phone, and an app that asked for everything — caste, income, disability, marital status, occupation — all linked to your Aadhaar number. The government called it "digital efficiency." Privacy advocates called it "the world's largest consent-free data harvest." The government disagreed with the term "consent-free," pointing out that citizens clicked "I Agree" on the terms and conditions. In 4-point font. On a mobile screen. At 11 PM. After an OTP that expired in 60 seconds.

## Meanwhile, at Railway Stations Across India

While 1.4 billion Indians were being asked to trust the Census app, a different kind of data collection was happening in plain sight.

Spanish company Herta Security — whose facial recognition technology is largely banned in the European Union under the AI Act — had quietly become the backbone of surveillance at Indian railway stations, temples, and urban centers. Over 4,000 cameras powered by Herta's algorithms were matching faces against databases. Which databases? Nobody said. Where did the data live? Nobody said. Who had access? Nobody said.

Herta received €3 million in EU research funding to develop this technology. The EU banned it at home for being too invasive. Then exported it to India, where no such restrictions existed. The technology was "developed in line with European data-protection principles," Herta said. It just couldn't *use* those principles at home.

India's legal experts debated whether this was a privacy violation or a sovereignty violation. It was, of course, both. European firms banned from scanning European faces were perfectly happy to scan Indian ones. Indian authorities were perfectly happy to be scanned. The citizens being scanned were not consulted.

This is what we now call the "Herta Doctrine": Your privacy is protected in the jurisdiction that can afford to protect it. Everywhere else, it's a market opportunity.

## The Consent Theatre

And then there was the DPDP Act — India's landmark data protection law, passed in 2023, rules notified in 2025, enforcement deadline set for May 2027. A beautifully drafted piece of legislation that existed entirely on paper for four years because the Data Protection Board of India — the enforcement body — was not constituted.

Think about that. For four years, Indian citizens had a data protection law with no one to enforce it. Companies were expected to comply with provisions that no regulator could penalize them for violating. It was like installing a burglar alarm and forgetting to plug it in. The alarm looks great on the wall. It just doesn't do anything.

By July 2026, the Board still didn't exist. The government, meanwhile, was busy launching a fully digital census that collected personal data from every citizen and linking it to Aadhaar. The same government that hadn't constituted the data protection board was collecting the most comprehensive dataset on Indian citizens ever assembled. The gap between privacy promise and privacy practice had never been wider.

MeitY did organize a "Code for Consent" innovation challenge. IDfy won with a consent management platform. This is the government equivalent of sponsoring a poetry contest about hunger while redirecting food subsidies. The consent was theatrical. The data collection was real.

## The Exclusion Report

But perhaps the cruelest joke of Census 2027 was the one played on the people who couldn't participate at all.

Enumerators faced "extreme heat, connectivity issues, app glitches, safety concerns, and resistance from residents." The self-enumeration portal required internet access and a smartphone — things that 35.6% of Indian women and significant rural populations didn't reliably have. India's digital divide, measured at 18% in 2023, had actually worsened to 31% by 2025 before improving to 14% by 2026. But "improvement" in aggregate numbers hides a brutal truth: the same people were being left behind every time — rural women, elderly citizens, Adivasi communities, and anyone without a smartphone.

The government celebrated that Census 2027 was "fully digital." The citizens who couldn't access the digital census were told to wait for enumerators. The enumerators were delayed by app glitches. The app glitches were caused by server overload from too many people trying to do what the government had made "optional" but clearly wanted everyone to do immediately.

A beautiful system. Circular. Exclusionary. And entirely predictable.

## What Was Actually Happening (Factual Summary)

1. **Census 2027 self-enumeration launched July 17, 2026**, with citizens encouraged to register household details on the official portal using Aadhaar-linked OTP. The process runs until July 31, followed by door-to-door verification. It is India's first fully digital census and first post-independence census to enumerate all castes.

2. **Investigate Europe and The Reporters' Collective revealed** that Spanish firm Herta Security, whose facial recognition technology is largely banned under the EU AI Act, powers over 4,000 cameras across India — at railway stations, temples, and in city-wide surveillance in Ahmedabad. The company received €3 million in EU research funding. Authorities have not disclosed which databases the systems check against.

3. **The Data Protection Board of India remains unconstituted** as of July 2026. The DPDP Act was passed in August 2023, rules notified in January 2025, and hard enforcement is not expected before May 2027. MeitY's "Code for Consent" innovation challenge was won by IDfy for its consent management platform.

4. **India's digital divide persists**, with internet access at 57.6% for men vs. 35.6% for women. The "fully digital" census model risks excluding precisely the populations most in need of being counted.

## Your Rights (This Is Not Satire)

You have the right to say no. Here's how:

- **Aadhaar is not mandatory for most services.** Supreme Court rulings (Puttaswamy, 2018; subsequent judgments) limit mandatory Aadhaar linking to government subsidies, PAN, and income tax. If a private entity demands Aadhaar, they must offer an alternative.

- **You can deny consent.** Under the DPDP Act (once enforceable), data fiduciaries must obtain *explicit, informed, and verifiable* consent. Clicking "I Agree" to unread terms does not constitute valid consent. If it's forced, it's coercion — not consent.

- **You can ask what data is collected and why.** The DPDP Act grants data principals the right to know what personal data is processed, the purpose, and the recipients. Exercise it.

- **Facial recognition without your knowledge may violate your fundamental right to privacy.** The Puttaswamy judgment established privacy as a fundamental right. If a surveillance camera is scanning your face without your knowledge or consent, the state must demonstrate a proportional, lawful purpose.

- **Report data misuse.** Once the DPDP Board is constituted, you can file complaints about data breaches, unauthorized sharing, or consent violations. Document everything. Screenshot the consent screen. Save the terms.

India's DPI stack is impressive. The ambition is real. But infrastructure without rights is just a faster way to violate people. Consent that isn't real isn't consent. A census that can't count everyone isn't a census. And a data protection law without a data protection board is a government confession that it doesn't intend to protect your data.

Remember: in 2026, they counted everything about you.

They just forgot to count your consent.
