## From the Memoirs of a Future Citizen

It was August 2026, and India was hosting the BRICS ICT Working Group in Pune. The theme was "Innovate, Cooperate and Transform (ICT) for a Resilient Future," which, translated from diplomatic English, meant: *please buy our digital infrastructure.*

Dr. Chandra Sekhar Pemmasani, the Union Minister of State for Communications, took the stage and delivered what was either a genuinely proud moment for Indian technology or the most audacious sales pitch in the history of governance tech. "Digital public infrastructure is not a luxury for developed economies," he told the assembled delegates from Brazil, South Africa, China, and the rest of the BRICS family. "It is a necessity for sustainable development across the Global South."

He offered India's DPI as a gift to the world. Modular. Open-source. No prohibitive licensing costs. Aadhaar for identity. UPI for payments. DigiLocker for documents. The full stack, ready to deploy, no assembly required.

What he did not mention — and to be fair, the delegates probably didn't ask — was that the country offering this "population-scale digital infrastructure" to the world had not yet staffed its own data protection regulator.

---

## The DPI Sales Pitch: Infrastructure Without Installation Manuals

Pemmasani's pitch was structurally sound, in the way that a bridge blueprint is structurally sound before anyone drives a truck over it. India's DPI stack had, by the government's own count, enabled billions of authentications, SIM issuances, and banking transactions. UPI processed over 14 billion transactions a month. Aadhaar had enrolled 145 crore people. On paper, this was a success story that any developing nation would want to replicate.

But here's the thing about selling infrastructure: you're supposed to include the maintenance manual.

India's Data Protection Board — the regulatory body established by the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — had been legally in existence since November 2025, when the DPDP Rules were notified. Eight months later, the Board remained, as LiveLaw put it, "established in law, absent in fact." The Search-cum-Selection Committee tasked with appointing a Chairperson had not managed to do so. The Board that was supposed to enforce ₹250 crore penalties for data breaches, adjudicate citizen complaints, and hold data fiduciaries accountable, was an empty room with a statutory mandate and no one sitting in the chairs.

The DPDP Act's substantive provisions — breach notification, consent obligations, security safeguards, the actual enforcement teeth — would not become operative until May 2027. Nearly four years after Parliament passed the Act. Three years after Presidential assent. Eight months after the Rules were notified. The board was a ghost.

So when India told BRICS nations, "Here, use our framework, it's open-source and modular," what it was really saying was: "Here's the car. We haven't installed the brakes yet. But look how fast it goes."

## Meanwhile, Back in Delhi: Facial Recognition Without a Script

While Pemmasani was in Pune selling digital governance to the world, Delhi Police were in the nation's capital demonstrating what Indian digital governance actually looked like on the ground.

MediaNama reported this week that Delhi Police had told the Supreme Court their facial recognition system "targets only people with criminal records." This was meant to be reassuring. It was not.

The problem — and it was a structural one, not a logistical one — is that facial recognition doesn't work like that. The technology doesn't *know* who has a criminal record until after it has scanned everyone's face and compared it against a database. You cannot "only scan criminals" with facial recognition. You scan *everyone* and then flag the matches. The police's statement to the Supreme Court was technically accurate in the same way that saying "we only arrest guilty people" is technically accurate — the determination of guilt comes *after* the universal scan, not before it.

An RTI application filed by MediaNama in February 2026 on Delhi Police's Republic Day AI facial-recognition glasses found no tenders, no approvals, no accountability guidelines, and no grievance-redressal process on record. In 2022, Delhi Police had told another RTI query that they had not assessed how facial recognition technology affects privacy. The DPDP Act, which might have governed this, had broad law enforcement exemptions and wouldn't be enforceable for another nine months.

A private member's bill — the Facial Recognition Technology (Regulation of Police Powers) Bill, 2023 — had proposed requiring magistrate authorisation before police could deploy facial recognition. It went nowhere. Petitioners from the Cockroach Janta Party had challenged the use of AI-powered smart glasses at Jantar Mantar protests as unconstitutional "indiscriminate biometric surveillance." The Delhi High Court had, at a July 2026 hearing, suggested the petitioner file a broader petition rather than issuing any binding order.

The surveillance state, in other words, was operating on a trust-based model. The police scanned your face. They promised they only looked at criminals. There were no rules saying they couldn't. There was no regulator to check. There was no privacy impact assessment. There was not even a procurement document explaining who built the system or what it cost.

India had no dedicated law governing biometric or facial-recognition surveillance. Not in 2022. Not in 2024. Not in August 2026. The world's largest democracy had deployed AI-powered face scanning on its citizens at protests, public events, and on city streets — and the only thing standing between a citizen and their biometric data was a police officer's promise.

## Exporting the Paradox: BRICS Gets the Stack, Not the Safeguards

This is the paradox at the heart of India's DPI export push, and it crystallised in a single week in August 2026.

On one hand, India was in Pune telling Brazil, South Africa, and the rest of the BRICS bloc: "Take our DPI. It's modular, open-source, and built for equity. It moves people out of poverty. It moves economies forward."

On the other hand, in Delhi, citizens were being scanned by facial recognition systems with no legal framework, no privacy assessment, no procurement documentation, and no regulatory oversight. The data protection law existed on paper but not in practice. The Data Protection Board existed in statute but not in person.

India was not exporting a regulatory model. It was exporting an *infrastructure* model. The distinction is crucial. Infrastructure is neutral. A road can be used to transport goods or to march an army. India's DPI — Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, the Account Aggregator — was built as public infrastructure. But public infrastructure deployed without regulatory guardrails is not a public good. It is a public liability.

The BRICS delegates in Pune heard about "national ownership and digital sovereignty." They were told each country could "retain control over their national systems, laws and data governance frameworks." What they were not told was that the country offering this advice had spent four years passing a data protection law, eight months after notifying its rules, and still could not manage to appoint a single person to enforce it.

## The Consent Theatre's New Act: Export-Grade Privacy

There was a dark comedy in the timing. The same week India was positioning itself as the Global South's digital infrastructure vendor, the Supreme Court had been asked to examine whether Delhi Police's use of facial recognition at protests was constitutional. The petitioners argued it was "indiscriminate biometric surveillance." The state's response amounted to: "We only scan the bad people. Trust us."

This is consent theater at its purest — not the theatrical consent of the Account Aggregator framework, where citizens click "I agree" without reading, but the constitutional consent of 1.4 billion people who were never asked whether they wanted their faces scanned at protests, their biometric data collected without statutory authority, or their digital infrastructure sold to other nations as a governance template.

India Stack Global — the government's initiative to promote India's DPI model internationally — was, in effect, packaging the country's most successful digital products and shipping them abroad with a label that read "open-source" but omitted the fine print: "privacy protections sold separately; regulatory enforcement not included; actual results may vary based on whether your country bothers to pass data protection laws faster than ours did."

## What Actually Happened This Week

**1. India hosts BRICS ICT Working Group, pitches DPI export.** Pune hosted the 7th BRICS ICT Working Group Meeting (Aug 17-21), with India proposing a BRICS repository for DPI solutions and offering to share its Aadhaar-UPI-DigiLocker stack as modular, open-source frameworks. Minister Pemmasani declared DPI "a necessity, not a luxury" for the Global South. India also hosted the 12th BRICS Communications Ministers Meeting on August 21.

**2. Delhi Police facial recognition faces SC scrutiny.** MediaNama reported that Delhi Police told the Supreme Court their FRT "targets only people with criminal records," while an RTI investigation found no tenders, approvals, privacy impact assessments, or grievance-redressal mechanisms for the Republic Day AI-powered facial recognition glasses. India has no dedicated law governing police use of facial recognition technology.

**3. Data Protection Board remains unstaffed.** LiveLaw reported that the Data Protection Board of India, established in law since November 2025 when the DPDP Rules were notified, remained without a Chairperson or members eight months later. The DPDP Act's substantive enforcement provisions, including breach notification and penalty powers, would not become operative until May 2027.

**4. DPDP Act challenged in Supreme Court.** Transparency activist Venkatesh Nayak and organisations including NCPRI and The Reporters' Collective filed petitions challenging the constitutionality of the DPDP Act and its amendment to the RTI Act. The Supreme Court issued notice in February 2026 but declined to grant an interim stay.

**5. IIT Gandhinagar develops AI face restoration.** A three-stage AI system from IIT Gandhinagar demonstrated the ability to reconstruct blurred or missing facial details from images in 30 seconds — a capability that, in the context of unchecked FRT deployment, raises additional questions about the reliability and evidentiary value of AI-processed biometric data.

## Know Your Rights


- **You have the right to ask *which* facial recognition system scanned you.** Under the RTI Act, you can file a query asking Delhi Police (or any state police force) for details of the FRT systems deployed, including procurement details, vendor names, and data retention policies. If they say "national security," challenge the specificity — blanket claims are not valid RTI exemptions.

- **Protest surveillance is constitutionally questionable.** If you believe you were subjected to facial recognition scanning at a protest or public gathering, document everything: date, time, location, any visible cameras or smart glasses, and file a complaint with the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC). The absence of a dedicated FRT law means the police are operating without statutory authority.

- **DPDP complaints: preserve your evidence now.** The Data Protection Board may be empty, but the legal right to data protection exists. If you've experienced unauthorised data sharing, non-consensual biometric collection, or a data breach that wasn't reported, document it with screenshots, timestamps, and correspondence. When the Board becomes functional (if ever), these records will be critical.

- **BRICS DPI adoption affects you too.** If India's DPI model is adopted by other nations, it normalises the same governance gaps globally. Write to your MP asking about India's export commitments: are we shipping regulatory frameworks alongside infrastructure, or just the infrastructure? Demand that India Stack Global include privacy guardrails, not just payment rails.

- **Challenge law enforcement data exemptions.** The DPDP Act's broad exemptions for state and law enforcement data processing are being challenged in the Supreme Court. Support organisations like the Internet Freedom Foundation, SFLC.in, and NCPRI who are litigating these issues. Public interest litigation needs public interest.
