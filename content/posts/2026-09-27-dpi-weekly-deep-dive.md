# DPI Weekly Deep Dive — Twenty Days to the Rate Card: MDR's Second Act and the Battle for UPI's Market Shape | Week of September 21–27, 2026

## Executive Summary

Last week this newsletter covered the *arrival* of the UPI Merchant Discount Rate — NPCI's September 15 notification of 0.4% on P2M transactions above ₹2,000, effective October 15, and the political storm that followed. This week the story entered its second act: the numbers, the winners, and the market shape. At the 13th SBI Banking & Economics Conclave in Mumbai, NPCI MD & CEO Dilip Asbe put hard figures on the recovery thesis: the 0.4% rate should pull in **₹13,000–15,000 crore in year one** against an estimated **₹21,000 crore annual cost** of running UPI, while **96% of transaction volumes and 75% of value** stay outside the charging net. Crucially, he conceded what zero-MDR defenders had long argued and monetisation advocates had long denied: ecosystem investment and UPI's growth *slowed* — to a forecast of roughly **10% value and 15–17% volume growth this year** — because "there was absolutely no revenue back in the system."

The government, meanwhile, filled in the plumbing. Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman told PTI the MDR is "not a tax, this is not a cess, this is not even a surcharge," collections do not touch the Consolidated Fund of India, and — for the first time officially — laid out the devolution split: **40% to the issuing bank, 30% to the acquirer or payment gateway, 20% to the UPI app, 10% to the payer's PSP bank**. A **0.02% concessional rate for capital-markets transactions** (mutual funds, stockbroking), also capped at ₹300, emerged as a previously unreported carve-out. And NPCI, in consultation with the RBI, is assembling a **₹3,000 crore fund over three years** — seeded by 5% of MDR collections — that could fund roughly **20 million soundboxes** and push card-accepting merchants toward 10 million, triggering what Business Standard called a merchant-network land grab by PhonePe, Paytm, Pine Labs and MobiKwik.

But the week's sharpest analysis came from outside the ecosystem. Reuters reported that PhonePe and Google Pay — which together carried **80% of UPI payment values in August** — stand to capture roughly **$900 million of the $1.1 billion** in annual app-level revenue that Bernstein expects the MDR to generate by March 2028, entrenching the incumbents months before NPCI's own 30% market-share cap on third-party apps is due for enforcement in December. The tension is now impossible to ignore: a rate card designed to fund a public rail will, on current market shape, deliver most of its rent to two private apps — and the regulator's answer to that concentration is itself scheduled to be tested in the same quarter. October 15 is no longer just a pricing deadline. It is the opening of a three-month window in which UPI's economics and UPI's market structure will be renegotiated in public.

## The Story in Depth

### Context

UPI's monetisation question has a ten-year arc and a well-documented recent chapter. Zero-MDR was imposed in January 2020 to accelerate adoption during a critical growth phase; it worked for volume and hollowed out the revenue model. The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance broke the taboo in March 2026 by recommending charges on large merchants; the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, introduced August 4, amended Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act to give the government legal room to notify which payment modes must stay free; and NPCI's September 15 circular delivered the rate card — 0.4% above ₹2,000, ₹300 cap at ₹75,000, flat ₹5 in essential sectors, P2P and small merchants untouched, surcharging and platform fees prohibited. Last week's deep dive catalogued the political fallout: the opposition's foreign-pressure narrative, the Finance Ministry's "patently false and misleading" rebuttal, and the brokerage scramble to model a ₹13,500–22,000 crore revenue pool. What remained unknown was the operator's own math, the official devolution split, and what the money would actually buy. All three arrived this week.

### What Happened This Week

**The operator's math (September 24–25).** Speaking at the SBI Banking & Economics Conclave, Asbe gave the most precise public accounting yet of UPI's cost crisis. The system costs an estimated **₹21,000 crore a year** to run; the 0.4% rate should recover **₹13,000–15,000 crore in the first year** — a deliberate partial recovery, not full cost recovery, sized to "incentivise market players to reinvest and grow UPI." The composition of the MDR pool matters as much as its size: about **80% will come from businesses with more than ₹1,000 crore in annual digital-payment collections**, which already pay 1.5–2% on card acceptance; another ~10% from businesses above ₹1 crore turnover; and only roughly **10% from merchants that do not accept credit cards at all** — the population where a new fee could genuinely change behaviour or get passed through. Asbe's own framing was disarming: "The price is already embedded with the charge." He also confirmed revenue would fund **capacity, cybersecurity, artificial intelligence and quantum-proofing**, noting NPCI has already exhausted much of its annual technology budget as the cost of some servers rose **fivefold to about ₹1 crore**.

**The growth-slowdown admission.** The most historically significant line of the week was almost a throwaway. Reinstating charges, Asbe said, had become necessary because "the initial five-six years of investments which we saw players making in the ecosystem kind of slowed down because there was absolutely no revenue back in the system." The current-year forecast — **about 10% growth in value and 15–17% in volume** — is a fraction of UPI's historical trajectory. For a decade, UPI's champions cited its growth curve as proof the free model worked; the operator has now formally entered into evidence the claim that the curve broke because the model was free. That admission will be cited in every future DPI-monetisation debate, in India and abroad.

**The official devolution split (September 25).** In a PTI interview, Sitharaman disclosed how the money divides: **40% issuing bank, 30% acquirer/payment gateway, 20% UPI app, 10% payer PSP bank, 0% government**. The issuing-bank-heavy structure mirrors pre-2020 card economics and validates the industry expectation that banks — including state-owned lenders that absorbed UPI's costs at scale — recapture the largest share. The interview also revealed a **0.02% concessional MDR for capital-markets transactions** (mutual funds, stockbroking), capped at ₹300, a carve-out that protects retail investment flows; industry commentary indicated recurring SIP mandates are fully exempt. RuPay debit transactions stay free, and Sitharaman reiterated that merchants — not consumers — bear the charge, with banks "advised to ensure" no pass-through.

**The concentration reckoning (September 22).** Reuters' analysis cut through the week's messaging: the end of free UPI hands PhonePe and Google Pay — **80% of August's UPI payment value between them** — the "firepower to expand to rural areas, while prompting rivals to chase higher-value transactions to draw users and fees." Bernstein's estimate: up to **$1.1 billion in annual revenue for payment apps by March 2028**, of which roughly **$900 million accrues to the two incumbents** at current share. PhonePe co-founder Rahul Chari called MDR "a positive step towards offsetting substantial operational costs and liabilities." The counterpart sentence is the one regulators should underline: the boost "could also renew focus on the level of market concentration regulators may be comfortable with" — with NPCI's 30% TPAP market-share cap, announced November 2020 and repeatedly deferred, due for enforcement in December 2026.

**The reinvestment land grab (September 24).** Business Standard reported that NPCI, in consultation with the RBI, is putting together a **₹3,000 crore fund over three years** to onboard merchants and build acceptance infrastructure in smaller cities. Asbe's quantification: "If ₹3,000 crore is given back as an incentive... it can easily fund the setting up of 20 million soundboxes and an additional 5 million card-accepting merchants." The acceptance-infrastructure gap is real — about **20 million merchants have soundboxes today while another 40 million have QR codes but no audio-confirm device**. The commercial response was instant: MobiKwik expects merchant revenue to treble in FY27 and is targeting a tenfold increase by FY28; Paytm is expanding its distribution team; PhonePe and Pine Labs are scaling device deployment. A reinvestment fund seeded by a public rate card is, in effect, underwriting a private merchant-acquisition race.

**The broader DPI canvas.** Three smaller threads rounded out the week. Asbe confirmed NPCI is in discussions with the RBI and expects to **launch agentic payments "in the next few months"**, positioning agentic commerce as a discoverability layer that could free small merchants from platforms charging "commissions of 20-30%" — while adopting, in his words, a cautious approach under RBI guidance. In Mint, tech-policy lawyer Rahul Matthan argued AI agents should get access to digital public infrastructure **only after passing a security check** — the first serious policy framing of agent-vs-DPI authentication as the Agentic UPI clock ticks. And at Sangam 2026 in Bengaluru, the Finance Minister unveiled *Cashless Nation: How UPI Changed Everything* (Penguin Random House), the official-history moment of UPI's decade — arriving, fittingly, in the same month the free era ended. Separately, DEA Secretary Anuradha Thakur confirmed India is advancing BRICS discussions on **cross-border payment interoperability and local-currency settlement**, the diplomatic track that UPI's monetisation model now underwrites.

### Why It Matters

Three structural points elevate this week beyond follow-on news. **First, the operator has published a cost-recovery ratio, not a profit target.** Recovering ₹13,000–15,000 crore of ₹21,000 crore is a deliberately partial socialisation of costs — the state is signalling that UPI remains subsidised infrastructure, just less so. This is a different creature from card networks' shareholder-return models, and it deserves to be defended as such rather than demagogued as a "tax." **Second, the pass-through question has an empirical boundary.** Asbe's own numbers — 80% of the pool from merchants already paying card MDR, only 10% from card-naive merchants — mean the anti-surcharging rule needs to hold hardest in exactly the segment where enforcement is weakest: the long tail of formalising retail. The Finance Ministry's monitoring commitment and the IBA's planned awareness campaign are the guardrails; the data from October 15 onward will judge them. **Third, the concentration paradox is now quantified.** A public rail's revenue flowing 90% to the duopoly at app layer (20% app share + a share of the acquirer 30% concentrated among apps that control acquiring relationships) sits awkwardly beside a 30% cap designed to prevent exactly this dominance. December's TPAP enforcement just became the most important payment-policy date of the quarter.

## Technical Deep Dive

The devolution split, now official, deserves precise statement:

| Participant | Share of MDR | Role in the flow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Issuing bank (payer's bank) | 40% | Bears fraud, credit and customer-service costs; card-era "interchange" analogue |
| Acquirer / payment gateway / merchant's bank | 30% | Merchant onboarding, settlement, device infrastructure |
| UPI app (TPAP) | 20% | Consumer surface, UX, distribution |
| Payer PSP bank (sponsoring the app) | 10% | Switching and PSP-layer operations |
| Government | 0% | Not a tax, cess or surcharge; nothing to the Consolidated Fund |

On a ₹5,000 transaction, the merchant pays ₹20 in UPI MDR against roughly ₹100 for a 2%-MDR credit card — the rate card's core pitch to merchants is "same acceptance, one-fifth the fee." The effective-rate curve from last week's analysis still governs: 0.4% between ₹2,000 and ₹75,000, declining above as the ₹300 cap binds, flat ₹5 in essential sectors, 0.02% in capital markets.

The fund mechanics close the loop: 5% of an expected ₹13,000–15,000 crore first-year MDR pool is **₹650–750 crore a year**, which compounds to roughly **₹2,000–2,250 crore over three years** — close enough to the announced ₹3,000 crore that the difference likely reflects growth in the charged base. Deployment targets: ~20 million soundboxes (audio-confirmation devices that de-risk QR acceptance for low-literacy and noisy environments), expanding card-accepting merchants from ~5 million toward 10 million, with priority for Tier 3–6 cities, the Northeast, Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh. This is the DPI cross-subsidy in action — the top 10% of merchant value funding the next 40 million merchants' infrastructure.

On the security side, Asbe's server-cost disclosure (fivefold rise to ~₹1 crore for some servers) and the quantum-proofing mention are the first public acknowledgments that UPI's infrastructure bill is inflating with adversary capability, not just volume — the substantive answer to "why charge now" that neither the August Bill nor the September circular articulated.

## Government Perspective

The government's messaging this week was disciplined and layered. Sitharaman's tax-denial formulation ("not a tax, not a cess, not even a surcharge") answers the opposition's political frame; the devolution disclosure answers the "where does the money go" miasma; and the RuPay-free commitment answers the US-trade-pressure narrative from the right flank. Finance Ministry sources went further, arguing merchants already absorb card MDR "within the same MRP," questioning why UPI should be exceptional, and fielding international comparisons: indicative acceptance charges of **0.22–0.33% in Brazil, ~0.30% in Indonesia, ~0.40% in China and South Africa**, higher in South Korea and Singapore, up to 0.5% in Australia and the US — placing India's 0.4% squarely mid-pack among instant-payment systems, though Pix's bank-set pricing shows a lighter-touch alternative exists. The Parliamentary Standing Committee's March recommendation is now the government's shield: this was legislated-in-the-open, committee-endorsed policy, not a bolt from the blue. The unresolved government posture is enforcement: the monitoring commitment on pass-through, the IBA awareness campaign, and a December TPAP-cap decision will define whether the state is merely a rate-setter or a market-shaper.

## Citizen Impact

For the consumer, the direct ledger is unchanged and genuinely favourable: P2P free at any amount, 96% of merchant transactions by volume untouched, surcharging banned, platform fees banned, capital-markets flows at 0.02% with recurring SIPs reportedly exempt. The indirect ledger is where citizens should watch. Pass-through, if it happens, will surface in categories where the >₹2,000 ticket meets thin margins and weak price competition — electronics, travel, repair services — not in the kirana's ₹300 sale. The flat ₹5 on railways, fuel, telecom and insurance above ₹2,000 is regressive by design; a monthly season-ticket buyer and an occasional traveller pay the same. Small merchants collecting under ₹1 lakh a month by QR stay fully exempt — but the incentive to *stay under* that line creates a reclassification economy (split invoices, cash-mixing, multiple QRs) that the ecosystem has not begun to police. The genuinely positive citizen story is the soundbox fund: audio confirmation reduces fraud disputes and builds trust for precisely the merchants and users the QR-only economy served worst. And the agentic-payments promise — merchant discoverability without 20–30% platform commissions — is the first articulation of DPI eating middleman rent rather than creating it, though Matthan's security-check caveat is the right note of caution: an agent with payment credentials is an attack surface the KYC framework never imagined.

## Global Context

The world is watching this experiment more closely than the domestic noise suggests. The IMF has recognised UPI as the world's largest real-time payment system; the DPI export agenda — BRICS payment interoperability discussions advancing this week, UPI live-linking in multiple countries — depends on demonstrating a *sustainable* public-rail economics. Brazil's Pix is the clearest comparator: no regulated rate card, merchant pricing left to banks, and an average cost near 0.22% that BIS economists documented as dramatically cheaper than cards. India has now chosen a more interventionist path — a published, capped, exemption-heavy tariff with an explicit reinvestment fund — that is closer to utility regulation than to either Pix's laissez-faire or card-network pricing. The choice has export consequences: a government-published rate card is evidence for the claim that public payment rails can be self-funding without becoming rent-extracting, which is precisely the argument Indian diplomats make when pitching DPI adoption abroad. Apple Pay's reported India launch next month, potentially with UPI support, adds a final wrinkle: global wallets entering a market in the same quarter its monetisation model inverts.

## Looking Ahead

The next ninety days are dense. **October 15**: go-live — watch merchant billing-system friction, the first reconciliation reports, and whether NPCI's "adequate lead time" holds at enterprise scale. **October–November**: pass-through monitoring — the Finance Ministry has committed to watching consumer burden; the first data will come from categories above ₹2,000 with price-sensitive demand. **December 2026**: the 30% TPAP cap enforcement decision — after this week's concentration analysis, deferral again would be indefensible silence, while enforcement would trigger the largest market-share redistribution in Indian payments since UPI launched. **Next few months**: agentic payments launch under RBI guidance, with the agent-authentication question Matthan raised as the unsolved design problem. And in the background, the small-merchant fund's first disbursements will test whether ₹3,000 crore of rate-card revenue can actually buy 20 million soundboxes — the first proof point that MDR is reinvestment, not just a levy. This newsletter will track all four, and the moment any of them moves, the daily brief will carry it first.
