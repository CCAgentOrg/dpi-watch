## I. One Billion Health Records, Zero Genuine Choices

**The News (June-July 2026):**

India's National Health Authority announced that over **100 crore (one billion) health records** are now linked to ABHA — Ayushman Bharat Health Accounts — under the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission. The number doubled from 50 crore to 100 crore in just 15 months. NITI Aayog celebrated the milestone. The National Health Authority called it a "significant acceleration." Karnataka began rolling out unified digital patient records in government hospitals, with **OTP-based consent access** — meaning your medical history is one missed phone call away from anyone who knows your number.

Simultaneously, Aarogya Setu 2.0 was relaunched — now bundled with PM-JAY hospital search, ABHA family profiles, blood bank locators, and AI-powered "smart health reports." The same app that was made mandatory during COVID-19, then quietly retired, then quietly un-retired — now repackaged as a health companion. With your data already inside.

**The Translation:**

> "Congratulations, India. One billion health records are now linked to a digital ID that most people created because a hospital registration desk said 'ABHA ID laga do, process fast hoga.' The consent? An OTP. The understanding of what they consented to? Also an OTP. The difference between informed consent and performative consent? We'll have a roundtable about that. Attendees only. Limited seats."

**The Satire:**

There is a beautiful symmetry to India's approach to digital health records: the government builds the infrastructure, the hospitals make it "convenient" to opt in, and the citizens provide "consent" through the same mechanism they use to verify a Zomato delivery — a six-digit OTP sent to a phone number that half the country shares with three family members.

Karnataka's new unified records system is a masterclass in consent architecture. Your medical history — diagnoses, prescriptions, lab reports, the embarrassing rash you got in 2019 that you told nobody about — is accessible via **OTP-based consent**. Let that sink in. The barrier between a stranger and your complete medical history is knowing your phone number and intercepting a text message. In a country where SIM card fraud is a cottage industry and PII breaches are announced monthly, this is the security model chosen for health data.

The Aarogya Setu 2.0 relaunch is equally thoughtful. India's pandemic contact-tracing app — once mandatory, once controversial, once "deprecated" — is back, rebranded as a health companion. The data it collected during COVID-19 never went away. It simply waited. Now it has new features: hospital search, AI health reports, family member linking. The pandemic app became the health app became the surveillance app, and nobody asked permission at any transition.

One billion records. Zero informed consent. This is not a digital health ecosystem. This is a data harvesting operation dressed in a sari of convenience.

## II. NPCI's Grand Plan: Let AI Decide Who Pays and When

**The News (June 27, 2026):**

Dilip Asbe, MD and CEO of the National Payments Corporation of India, told TechCrunch at Mumbai Tech Week that **AI will be "heavily involved" in the next era of UPI growth.** With over 750 million daily UPI transactions and a target of one billion, Asbe envisions AI driving the next half billion users through multilingual interfaces, voice assistants, fraud prevention, and credit distribution. Voice payments, he noted, are the next frontier — once voice models become more accurate.

The same week, IAMAI held a closed-door "Data Protection Dialogue" in New Delhi to discuss the "practicalities of DPDP implementation" — specifically consent architecture, verifiable parental consent, and cross-border data transfers. "Limited seats only," the invitation said. The DPDP Act's core obligations take effect in May 2027, and India Inc. is scrambling to figure out what consent even means.

**The Translation:**

> "We have 750 million daily UPI transactions and we want a billion. Our plan? Have AI handle the next 500 million users — including voice payments for people who don't read the language on their screen. Don't worry about consent. We're having a roundtable about it next month. Very exclusive. You wouldn't understand it anyway — it's for legal people."

**The Satire:**

The NPCI's AI vision for UPI is a Rube Goldberg machine of consent erosion disguised as financial inclusion.

India has already moved from "I pay" to "I approve" to "my AI agent pays on my behalf" (Pine Labs' P3P protocol, launched weeks ago). Now the NPCI wants AI to onboard the *next half billion users* — people who may not read English, may not understand digital interfaces, and may not even know they're being onboarded. Voice payments, Asbe suggests, are the answer. Because nothing says "informed financial consent" quite like a person speaking in Kannada to an AI voice model that may or may not transcribe "send ₹500" as "send ₹50,000."

The fraud angle is equally rich. Asbe says AI will improve fraud prevention. This is the same NPCI whose network processes 750 million daily transactions and where UPI fraud complaints have risen year-on-year — because when you move money at the speed of light, fraud moves faster. AI detecting AI-generated fraud is a cybersecurity ouroboros, and the citizen is the meat inside the snake.

Meanwhile, India's corporate elite gathered at The Oberoi to discuss DPDP "consent architecture" over chai and canapés. The irony writes itself: the people designing India's consent framework are the same people who spent the last decade building businesses on the assumption that consent was a checkbox, not a conversation. Now that the law says consent must be "specific, informed, and unambiguous," they need a "dialogue" to figure out what those words mean.

Here's a hint: they mean what 1.4 billion Indians think they mean — that you ask before you take, you explain before you collect, and "I consented to create an ABHA ID at a hospital" does not mean "I consented to my health records being shared with every ABDM-compliant facility in the country."

## III. The DPDP Act: A Privacy Law Written by People Who Don't Want Privacy

**The News (June 2026):**

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, with its implementing rules notified in 2025, is moving into phased enforcement. The Data Protection Board has been constituted. Organisations have until May 2027 for full compliance. IAMAI hosted its "Data Protection Dialogue" — limited seats, The Oberoi, New Delhi — to discuss "practicalities." ORF published an issue brief on EdTech data risks under the DPDP Act, noting that the law "does not provide specific operational standards." A LinkedIn post by a privacy consultant warned of penalties "running into hundreds of crores" for non-compliance.

**The Translation:**

> "We wrote a privacy law. It says you need consent. What does consent mean? We'll figure that out. When? Before May 2027, hopefully. In the meantime, here's a ₹250 crore penalty if you get it wrong. What is 'it'? We'll let you know. Please form a queue at The Oberoi. Canapés are on the house. The house being your data."

**The Satire:**

The DPDP Act is India's privacy law in the same way that a blank cheque is a payment instrument — technically correct, practically useless until someone fills in the details.

The Act says consent must be "specific, informed, and unambiguous." The 2025 Rules say organisations must provide notices. But neither document defines what "informed" actually looks like when a user clicks "Accept" on a 47-page terms-of-service document written in English legalese, displayed on a 6-inch phone screen, while a hospital receptionist hovers impatiently. The ORF brief on EdTech data risks — focused on children, no less — acknowledges the gap: the DPDP Act "does not provide specific operational standards." For children. The most vulnerable digital citizens. The law protecting them has no operational standards.

This is not a bug. This is the design. India's digital public infrastructure philosophy has always been: **build first, regulate later, and if regulation threatens the build, regulate just enough to claim you regulated.** Aadhaar was "voluntary" until it was mandatory. DigiLocker was "optional" until government services required it. ABHA was "for your convenience" until hospitals made it the only way to register. The DPDP Act will be "world-class privacy protection" until the government exempts itself — which it already has, via Section 17, which allows the state to process personal data without consent for "specified purposes."

The Data Protection Board — India's answer to GDPR's supervisory authority — has been constituted. Its members will adjudicate complaints, investigate breaches, and impose penalties. Whether it will have the independence, resources, or political will to hold the government's own DPI systems accountable remains the most entertaining unanswered question in Indian technology policy.

## What Citizens Can Actually Do

1. **Opt out where you can.** ABHA accounts can be deactivated. UPI transactions can be limited. DigiLocker documents can be deleted. You will be told this is "inconvenient." That is the point.

2. **Read the consent before you click.** Yes, all 47 pages. Yes, in English. Yes, while the receptionist hovers. This is the only leverage you have — the system depends on nobody reading the terms.

3. **Use the Data Protection Board.** Once operational, file complaints. It exists for a reason (theoretically). Even if it's underfunded and understaffed, a flood of complaints creates political pressure.

4. **Separate your health data from your identity.** Do not link ABHA to every hospital visit. Do not use Aarogya Setu 2.0 if you can avoid it. Your medical history is not the government's infrastructure project.

5. **Demand specific consent, not blanket consent.** When a hospital says "ABHA laga do," ask: "What records will this link? Who can access them? How do I unlink them?" If they can't answer, don't link it.
