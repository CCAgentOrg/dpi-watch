## My Body, Their Terms

In 2026, I kept three proofs of personhood: a cylinder number, a ration card, and the face I had been issued at birth. The first two were useful. The third was apparently a legacy format.

At the LPG counter, the refill had become a small seminar in public policy. The government said biometric Aadhaar authentication would stop subsidy misuse. A lawyer with a visual disability told the Delhi High Court that iris scanning and other biometric checks could make the process difficult for him and others unable to complete it. The Court asked the Centre, the petroleum regulator, and oil companies to respond. It had not decided the case; the next hearing was still ahead.[^1]

I asked the clerk what to do if the scanner could not read me.

“Stand still,” he said.

“I am standing.”

“Then stand more authentically.”

This was not a joke the clerk made. It was a joke the system made through him. The court was still hearing whether the mandate had legal backing, while the practical question arrived first: if I could not authenticate, did I still get the refill at the regulated price? The filing said the requirement affected 3.16 crore consumers. The number was a petitioner’s claim, not a court finding, but it was large enough to make the word “exception” feel like a very small door.[^1]

The state called this verification. My body called it Tuesday.

The ration department offered a more comforting interface. Karnataka’s Anna Seve app let Public Distribution System beneficiaries complete e-KYC without travelling to a fair-price shop or government office. For people who had moved to cities for work, that could save a long trip and a queue. It was an actual convenience, not merely a keynote slide. But the process also came with a deadline: October 20. People who had completed e-KYC more than a year earlier had to repeat it. On October 12, the department said 2,06,76,278 of 2,67,64,276 beneficiaries had completed the process; 1,60,267 had used the app in the pilot districts.[^2]

I completed mine on my phone while it had a signal, battery, storage, an updated app, and the cheerful cooperation of the camera. The app saved me a trip. It did not abolish the deadline; it made the deadline portable.

“Is the app optional?” I asked the fictional helpline.

“Certainly,” said the operator. “You may also complete e-KYC in person.”

“Where?”

“At the office you were trying not to travel to.”

This was called choice: two routes to the same requirement, one in your hand and one across town. I was grateful for the first route. I also noticed that gratitude had been listed as a system requirement.

At the airport, I met a more advanced version of the same conversation. Digi Yatra had been collecting personal data for identity verification. In earlier directions, the Kerala High Court said it could not insist on Aadhaar details and noted that a driving licence was accepted; the Bench said authorities could consider Voter ID too. In October, while addressing a petition about passenger data handling and onward sharing, the Court directed the Union government to make the Data Protection Board functional within eight weeks. It observed that concerns about privacy could not be ignored.[^3]

Eight weeks is a humane amount of time if you are waiting for a committee. It is a long time if your personal data is already on a conveyor belt labelled “service improvement.” The petition sought to stop commercial licensees and airport intermediaries from sharing or commercially exploiting passenger data without authorisation. The existence of a data-protection law was not the same thing as an available place to complain about how data travelled. The Court’s order was the clearest sign that the remedy was still catching up with the collection.[^3]

I asked the airport kiosk whether I could choose another identity document.

“You can,” it said. “We accept a driving licence.”

“And a voter card?”

“We may consider it in the future.”

“Can I choose not to have my passenger data shared?”

“Please see the privacy policy.”

“Can I choose not to accept the privacy policy?”

“Please see the service.”

The policy was a twenty-page scroll with one blue button at the bottom. The button said “Continue.” There was no button saying “Continue, but let me know which company gets my data.” That would have required a second screen, and the airport was already full of people trying to be somewhere else.

### Bureaucratic Testimonial

*“No citizen is compelled to use a digital service. The citizen is simply required to complete the service digitally, unless they choose the equivalent physical process, which may involve travel, queues, and office hours. This preserves choice while ensuring the choice is exercised correctly.”* — **Fictional statement, Department of Voluntary Requirements, 2031**

Looking back, the clever part was not that every system demanded the same number. It was that each system could describe its own demand as a narrow administrative convenience: a refill safeguard here, a repeat e-KYC there, an identity check at the airport. Each case had its own rationale. The accumulated citizen was the one asked to remember every rationale, every deadline, every exception, and every grievance route.

That is how “voluntary” survives contact with a queue. It does not have to mean “you must.” It can mean “you may decline, and then discover what the service was attached to.” The app can save a journey; biometrics can reduce some forms of fraud; interoperable identity can make a transaction faster. None of those benefits answers the next questions: what happens when authentication fails, who can see the data, how long is it kept, and where can a person obtain a remedy?

In 2031, my old devices have all been replaced. The questions remain compatible with every new version.

## Factual Summary of the Events Satirized

This week’s reporting covered three separate matters: (1) on October 14, the Delhi High Court issued notices in a PIL challenging biometric Aadhaar authentication for subsidised LPG refills; (2) Karnataka expanded the Anna Seve app for PDS e-KYC statewide, with an October 20 deadline and repeat e-KYC for checks older than a year; and (3) the Kerala High Court directed the Data Protection Board to become functional within eight weeks while considering passenger-data practices linked to Digi Yatra. The LPG case remains pending; the e-KYC figures and the 3.16-crore estimate are attributed to the department and petitioner respectively; the satire’s officials and dialogue are fictional.[^1][^2][^3]

## Actionable Citizen Rights Advice

- **For subsidised LPG:** If authentication fails or is inaccessible, ask the distributor for the current written rule and the available alternative process. Keep the date, receipt, and complaint reference. The reported court matter is pending; do not treat the petition’s arguments as a final ruling.
- **For PDS e-KYC:** Check the state department’s instructions and deadline; keep proof of completion. If an app or biometric check fails, document the error and ask the fair-price shop or district food-supply office how to complete verification without losing access while the issue is resolved.
- **For airport identity checks:** Ask which non-Aadhaar document is accepted and read the current privacy notice before sharing data. Avoid sending Aadhaar images or OTPs to unofficial intermediaries. Keep a copy of any complaint about data use or disclosure.
- **For all three:** Ask for the legal or administrative basis, the data recipient, retention period, and grievance route in writing. If an essential benefit is denied, preserve the evidence and seek help from the relevant department’s grievance channel or a local legal-aid service.
