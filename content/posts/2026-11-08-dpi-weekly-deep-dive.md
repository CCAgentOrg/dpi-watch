# DPI Weekly Deep Dive — The Payment Rails Get a Security Rulebook: RBI, CERT-In and the Agentic-AI Threat | Week of November 1–8, 2026

## Executive Summary

This week, India's payment infrastructure got the most concentrated dose of security regulation in its history — and the trigger was a threat that moves at machine speed. A confidential Reserve Bank of India advisory dated November 3, issued through its Cyber Security and IT Risk (CSITE) Group after a meeting with bankers, now requires scheduled commercial and urban co-operative banks that offer API-based fund transfers to non-banking financial companies to maintain full API client inventories, submit to CERT-In-empanelled security audits before onboarding new NBFC clients, enforce per-transaction and daily limits, and stand up beneficiary whitelisting and maker-checker controls on payout APIs, with a hard deadline of December 3, 2026 for existing integrations.[^1][^2][^3] Two days later, RBI followed with a consolidated Master Direction merging cyber-resilience and digital-payment-security requirements for the non-bank side of the rails — payment aggregators, prepaid instrument issuers, card networks and white-label ATM operators — including a six-hour breach-reporting clock, mandatory device binding, and a minimum twelve-hour cooling period before online transactions work on an instrument whose registered mobile number or email has just been changed.[^4]

The regulatory wave answers a live campaign. CERT-In's Computer Security Incident Response Team for Finance has designated the threat actor as "CRAFTY FALCON": attackers using AI-driven offensive tools — the ARTEX family of LLM-based tools, the PentAGI autonomous multi-agent penetration-testing system, the Cairn attack search engine and the CyberStrikeAI framework — to find exposed staging servers, ride shared credentials into production, and invoke fund-disbursement APIs directly, validating flows with ₹1 micro-transactions before draining real money through merchant settlement APIs and mule accounts.[^5][^6] The Economic Times has reported roughly ₹300 crore siphoned from smaller NBFCs over about two months and more than 150 FIRs, while the Central Bank of India separately disclosed ₹13.97 crore lost through its financial messaging system in October.[^7][^8] "The threat is active and ongoing," CERT-In's November 4 advisory states — including successful attacks on institutions that had already received restricted advisories and remediated.[^5]

This is the week's most consequential DPI story because it hardens the connective tissue that every other layer of digital public infrastructure depends on: the programmatic interfaces through which welfare disbursements, loan payouts, merchant settlements and bill payments actually move. The same week also showed why the stakes are rising rather than falling — NPCI announced its fourth subsidiary, NPCI Tech Solutions Limited, to institutionalise AI and blockchain work for India's DPI programmes,[^9] and Cashfree Payments launched a checkout layer that lets AI shopping agents read and initiate payments.[^10] India is simultaneously building agentic AI into its payment rails and defending those rails against agentic attacks, with binding rules arriving faster than almost any comparable jurisdiction. The unresolved question, developed below, is whether supervision is keeping pace with speed — and whether citizens will ever hear directly when the rails carrying their money are breached.

## The Story in Depth

### Context: the invisible API layer of India's payment DPI

Public discussion of India's digital public infrastructure tends to focus on the visible layers: the UPI app on a phone, the QR code on a shop counter, the Aadhaar number on a form. Beneath those sits a much less visible layer — machine-to-machine APIs through which banks connect to NBFCs for loan disbursement, payment aggregators connect to merchant settlement systems, and fintechs connect to the core banking systems that actually move money. Every welfare payout routed through a lending app, every "instant loan" credited in minutes, every merchant settlement arrives through this plumbing. CERT-In's October 9 advisory (CIAD-2026-0046), a "Critical" notice to NBFCs, digital lenders, fintechs, payment service providers and wallet operators, describes how attackers compromised internet-facing applications to steal payment-gateway and partner-bank API credentials, then invoked fund-transfer APIs *from entities' own whitelisted infrastructure* — bypassing application-level controls including KYC checks, beneficiary verification and transaction limits, because the requests looked like they came from inside.[^11]

That is the specific failure mode the week's rules target. A follow-up CERT-In advisory (CIAD-2026-0049, October 15) had already warned that financially motivated attackers were conducting campaigns against Indian entities "using publicly available, AI-native offensive security frameworks."[^12] The scale reported is material: the Economic Times, citing highly placed sources, reported about ₹300 crore stolen from smaller NBFCs — those with turnover of roughly ₹1,000 crore or less — over about two months, with more than 150 FIRs filed; in one case a Delhi-based NBFC lost ₹7.5 crore and another ₹7.25 crore days later; investigators have traced some attacks to CyberStrike, described as a Chinese hacking platform, with attackers combining the Artex and CyberStrike toolkits with open-source models such as DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi and GLM.[^7] These are media-reported figures: neither CERT-In's public advisories nor RBI notices confirm the amounts, a disclosure gap examined below. Separately, the Central Bank of India told police that hackers siphoned ₹13.97 crore through its financial messaging system in October, with 187 transfers untraceable;[^8] SBI disclosed that data of 3,897 customers had been exposed and surfaced on the dark web; and a microfinance NBFC, Spandana Sphoorty, disclosed ₹7.8 crore moved out of a company bank account on November 1.[^7]

The scale of what these APIs carry is worth stating plainly, because it is the denominator of the risk. UPI processed 25.47 billion transactions worth ₹31.53 lakh crore in October — the first month above ₹30 lakh crore in value, with average daily value crossing ₹1 lakh crore for the first time, roughly 821 million transactions a day.[^13] The record landed with a sobering quality signal: NPCI's September data shows technical decline rates among the twenty largest remitter banks rose to 0.24% from 0.18%, with ICICI Bank at 0.73% (from 0.03%), SBI at 0.38% (from 0.10%), Kotak Mahindra at 0.36% (from 0.02%) and Indian Overseas Bank at 0.24% (from 0.06%)[^14] — evidence that the bank-side systems terminating payment traffic are under stress even before counting the adversarial load described by CERT-In.

### What happened this week: four instruments in five days

The week's regulatory sequence deserves reconstruction, because its parts interlock.

**November 3 — the RBI API advisory.** After a meeting with bankers, RBI's CSITE Group issued a confidential advisory to scheduled commercial banks and urban co-operative banks offering API-based fund transfers to NBFCs, whether directly or through intermediaries and third-party service providers.[^1][^2] Its requirements, reported by MediaNama, The Head & Tale and Elets BFSI: maintain a complete inventory of API clients — business profile, services used, account and integration details, transaction limits, and key business, technical and security contacts; enforce per-transaction and daily cumulative limits immediately, with intra-day velocity limits within a month; reject payouts to unregistered beneficiary accounts and allow additions only through a separate, multi-factor-authenticated channel (beneficiary whitelisting); and apply maker-checker or out-of-band confirmation to API debits, with at least one such safeguard live for all existing integrations by December 3, 2026. NBFC clients using bank APIs must undergo security audits by CERT-In-empanelled auditors — before onboarding for new clients, and by February 3, 2027 for existing ones — with audits repeated at least annually and after any material cyber incident.[^1][^2][^3]

**November 3–5 — the I4C and CERT-In advisories.** The same day, the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre's National Cybercrime Threat Analysis Unit, together with CERT-In, publicly warned banks, NBFCs and fintechs that attackers are draining automated disbursement systems and payment APIs, with stolen funds pushed through mule accounts via IMPS and NEFT and records deleted to stall investigations; institutions were directed to implement the two October advisories, inventory and decommission old payment APIs, and honour the six-hour incident-reporting window.[^15] On November 4, CERT-In's finance arm escalated with its CRAFTY FALCON advisory, naming the AI-driven offensive toolset for the first time and documenting the attack anatomy in unusual detail: abuse of AWS Systems Manager and Lambda for command execution; movement from internet-exposed testing and staging environments into production through shared credentials, tokens and network paths; reverse-engineered application codebases repackaged to fire unauthorised API calls; IP allow-listing bypassed by routing traffic through shared third-party fetcher pools such as content-acceleration and app-store review services; ₹1 micro-value transactions to validate flows before larger ones; and exploitation of merchant settlement APIs alongside newly disclosed PHP and Node.js vulnerabilities.[^5][^6] Its recommendations are a de facto engineering standard: isolate staging from production, eliminate shared credentials, server-side transaction validation, API request signing, app attestation, certificate pinning, rapid patching, monitoring for micro-value-then-high-value patterns, beneficiary cooling periods, and maker-checker approvals for merchant settlements.[^6]

**November 4 — CERT-In's AI Security Auditing Guidelines.** In parallel, CERT-In released guidelines establishing a four-tier risk framework for auditing AI systems in India — low, moderate, high and critical — with moderate-risk systems requiring annual independent audits by CERT-In-empanelled auditors, high-risk systems facing assessments possibly every six months, and critical-risk systems requiring empanelled audits plus continuous monitoring.[^16] The guidelines specify human-oversight models (human-in-the-loop, on-the-loop, over-the-loop), require contestability mechanisms so affected individuals can seek review of AI-assisted decisions, set remediation timelines with monthly progress reporting for critical findings, and add supplementary requirements for generative AI, LLMs, RAG systems and agentic and multi-agent AI — explicitly covering prompt injection, data and model poisoning, and sensitive-information disclosure.[^16] This is the audit scaffolding for the technology both sides of the fraud ledger now use.

**November 5 — the Master Direction for non-bank payment operators.** Reported publicly on November 7, RBI's consolidated Master Direction merges older instructions on third-party ATM switch providers and payment-system audits into a single cyber-resilience and digital-payment-security rulebook for payment aggregators, prepaid payment instrument issuers, card networks and white-label ATM operators.[^4] The consumer-relevant clauses: six hours to report cyberattacks, critical-infrastructure outages, internal fraud and settlement delays; device binding on payment instruments; automatic termination of inactive sessions; detection or blocking of remote-access apps while a payment is in use; and a minimum twelve-hour cooling period before online transactions work on an instrument whose registered mobile number or email has been changed — a direct anti-SIM-swap tripwire. Governance rises to the board through a technology-risk committee meeting at least quarterly; disaster recovery must sit in a different seismic zone with drills twice a year and near-zero recovery-point objectives; annual system audits are due within two months of financial year-end, with a three-month cap on remediating open observations. Large and medium operators must comply immediately; small operators have until April 1, 2028.[^4]

**The same week, on the other side of the ledger.** On November 4, NPCI announced NPCI Tech Solutions Limited (NTSL), its fourth wholly owned subsidiary after NPCI International Payments, NPCI Bharat BillPay and NPCI BHIM Services, formed with board and RBI approval to work on AI, blockchain, digital financial infrastructure and global capability centre services — and "to provide tech services for several of India's digital public infrastructure programmes," under MD & CEO Vishal Kanvaty, previously NPCI's chief technology officer.[^9] On November 6, Cashfree Payments launched "Checkout for Agents," a WebMCP-based layer letting AI shopping agents read a checkout page's order amount, items and payment methods directly rather than scraping pixels, cutting token consumption by over 40% — with final payment approval retained by the customer on their own device.[^10] And NPCI filed its counter-affidavit in the Supreme Court defending the UPI MDR framework, arguing the 0.4% fee touches only about 4% of person-to-merchant payments above ₹2,000, that merchants are strictly prohibited from passing it on, and that 5% of every entity's MDR share funds small-merchant adoption; the go-live itself remains unnotified, with reports pointing to a possible January 1, 2027 start.[^17] The infrastructure is being monetised, extended to agents, and defended in court in the same week it is being hardened against machine-speed attack — four institutional responses to the same underlying fact: the rails are now critical national infrastructure.

### Why it matters: governance catching up to machine speed

The deeper significance of the week is the *form* of the response. India's fintech regulation has historically favoured principles, licensing gates and advisory circulars. These instruments are different: they are prescriptive, deadline-bound, and written against specific attack anatomy — the staging-to-production credential path, the ₹1 test transaction, the settlement API called from whitelisted infrastructure. The RBI advisory's insistence on a *complete API client inventory* addresses the most common failure in the reported incidents: institutions that did not know which interfaces they had exposed, or which had been decommissioned in name only. The CERT-In guidelines' tiering of AI systems, and their explicit treatment of agentic AI as a distinct audit subject, begin to build the oversight vocabulary for systems that act rather than merely answer.[^16]

But the week also exposed the gap between supervisory speed and public accountability. The most consequential instruments — the November 3 RBI advisory, the October CERT-In advisories that preceded it, the restrictions behind them — are confidential or generic. MediaNama's running accounting of the episode is pointed: the ₹300 crore loss estimate, the 150 FIRs, the Central Bank of India's ₹13.97 crore and SBI's dark-web exposure all became public through journalism — police complaints, exchange filings made after media reports, anonymous sources — not through regulator disclosure. Public advisories name no affected entities, publish no loss figures, and state no incident counts.[^7] India has a six-hour incident-reporting regime for regulators (CERT-In's 2022 directions, RBI's July 2026 NBFC cybersecurity directions through DAKSH, SEBI's six-hour clock), but no comparable obligation running to citizens.[^7] The DPDP Act's breach-notification rules — detailed reports to the Data Protection Board within 72 hours and notice to every affected individual "without delay" in plain language — take effect only on May 13, 2027, and then only for personal-data breaches, not necessarily for the fund-theft incidents at issue.[^7] Until then, the citizen learns that the rails were robbed from the newspaper.

## Technical Deep Dive

It is worth setting out the anatomy of the CRAFTY FALCON campaign and the countermeasures now mandated, because together they describe what secure payment DPI must look like going forward.[^5][^6]

The attack chain, as documented by CERT-In: reconnaissance of internet-exposed, non-production environments (test and staging systems that frequently share credentials and network paths with production); abuse of cloud management tooling — AWS Systems Manager and serverless functions — to execute commands and stage malicious files without touching traditional malware signatures; reverse-engineering of application codebases to craft tailored exploits delivered through modified application packages; bypassing of IP allow-listing by routing requests through shared third-party fetcher IP pools, including content-acceleration and app-store review services whose traffic institutions had trained themselves to trust; validation of unauthorised fund flows through micro-value transactions such as ₹1 transfers; and execution of larger transfers through merchant settlement APIs and disbursement APIs, with stolen funds laundered through mule accounts on IMPS and NEFT rails. Anti-forensic behaviour — log deletion, EDR tampering, and timing attacks to weekends and holidays, including Diwali week itself — appears throughout.[^5][^6][^15]

The mandated countermeasures map to each stage. API client inventories and decommissioning of unused interfaces shrink the attack surface before attackers reach it. Environment isolation and elimination of shared credentials cut the staging-to-production path. API request signing (nonces and timestamps that make replay infeasible), app attestation and certificate pinning raise the cost of invoking APIs from modified packages. Server-side transaction validation ensures client-side checks are never the last line. Beneficiary whitelisting with MFA-gated additions and cooling periods slows the money-movement phase, the stage where minutes determine whether funds are recoverable. Maker-checker and out-of-band confirmation on API debits, per-transaction and velocity limits, and monitoring for the micro-value-then-high-value signature target the machine-speed escalation that distinguishes this campaign from earlier fraud. The Master Direction's device binding, session termination, remote-access-app detection and SIM-change cooling period extend the same logic to the consumer endpoint, where SIM-swap fraud has been the dominant retail vector.[^1][^4][^6]

The CERT-In AI Security Auditing Guidelines add the audit layer: four risk tiers determining audit depth and frequency; three human-oversight models whose appropriateness must itself be assessed; contestability mechanisms for affected individuals; defined remediation timelines (a plan within 30 business days and implementation within 60 for moderate findings; monthly progress reporting for critical ones); re-audit triggers including two or more critical findings within three months; and supplementary requirements for agentic and multi-agent AI covering prompt injection, poisoning and sensitive-information disclosure.[^16] For DPI programmes adopting AI — fraud-scoring models, credit underwriting, chatbot grievance interfaces, and whatever NTSL builds — this framework will increasingly define what "audited" means in Indian infrastructure.

## Government Perspective

The week reveals a deliberate supervisory sequence rather than a scramble. The Reserve Bank's October 19 advisory had already told regulated entities to treat their technology environments as compromised until forensically cleared;[^18] the November 3 banker meeting produced the API advisory within hours; the Master Direction followed on November 5, extending the same architecture to non-banks; CERT-In and I4C published public advisories in parallel; and CERT-In trained more than 300 officials from over 55 financial institutions — predominantly regional rural and co-operative banks — in an October 27 drill.[^7][^15] The policy intent is clear: lift the weakest links (small NBFCs, co-operative lenders, small payment operators) to a common floor before the agentic threat landscape matures further.

The government's choices also encode its read of the trade-offs. Deadlines are aggressive — December 3, 2026 for maker-checker on existing integrations, February 3, 2027 for NBFC audits — but differentiated, with small operators granted until April 1, 2028 under the Master Direction.[^1][^4] Confidentiality of the bank advisory protects operational detail from attackers, but sits uneasily beside the public interest in knowing systemic losses; the media-reported ₹300 crore figure remains officially unconfirmed.[^7] The honest assessment is that India's supervisory apparatus moved faster this fortnight than in any comparable episode — eMudhra's cryptographic failure just two weeks earlier produced advisories and root-store action, and this week produced binding rules — while its public-disclosure apparatus moved barely at all. Both facts are true, and the second materially weakens the first, because oversight without transparency cannot be corrected by the public that bears the risk.

## Citizen Impact

For most Indians the effects will arrive as friction, and the friction is largely the point. Expect payout delays and extra confirmation steps when fintech apps send money to newly added bank accounts — that is beneficiary whitelisting and maker-checker working as designed. Expect a twelve-hour pause on online payments after a SIM or email change on any non-bank payment instrument: if you *made* that change, it is an annoyance; if you did not, it is the window in which you can call the provider before a fraudster can transact — the single most directly protective clause in the week's instruments.[^4] If a transfer you did not authorise hits your account, the advisory logic is explicit that minutes matter: call the bank's fraud line and the national cybercrime helpline 1930, and report at cybercrime.gov.in, because the six-hour clocks that now bind institutions reflect how quickly money becomes unrecoverable.[^15]

The record October's quality data matters here too. At 821 million transactions a day, even a 0.24% system-wide technical decline rate means roughly two million failed payments daily; on ICICI, SBI and Kotak rails the September rates imply failures several times more likely than in August. Money debited on failed transactions must be auto-refunded within T+1, and the failure is more likely the bank's systems than the user's app — consumers should chase their bank, not the merchant.[^13][^14] Two gaps deserve citizen attention. First, the six-hour reporting clock runs from operator to regulator, not to affected users: there is still no rule requiring a payment operator to tell users directly that a breach touched them, and the DPDP individual-notice obligation arrives only in May 2027. Second, the coming agentic checkout — Cashfree's launch among the first — places the approval screen as the only checkpoint that is actually the customer's;[^10] a manipulated agent can complete a payment faster than a human can review it, which is precisely the trust problem the eMudhra incident posed for websites, relocated to payments.

## Global Context

India is not alone in confronting machine-speed fraud, but it is regulating the agentic threat faster than most. The European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act has bound financial entities to ICT risk-management and incident-reporting obligations since January 2025, but addresses AI-mediated attacks only through general resilience language; standard-setting bodies are still catching up — the Basel Committee's CPMI-IOSCO cyber-resilience guidance for financial market infrastructures was only recently issued in consultative form, and the Financial Stability Board's common format for incident reporting exchange (FIRE) is likewise nascent.[^19] The United Kingdom's mandatory reimbursement regime for authorised push-payment fraud, live since October 2024, attacks the harm from the compensation side rather than the control side. India's approach this week — prescribing specific controls (request signing, whitelisting, maker-checker, device binding, SIM-change cooling) against a named, documented attack chain, with compliance deadlines in months rather than years — is more prescriptive than any of these, and its AI-audit tiering with CERT-In-empanelled auditors has no direct international analogue. The open question is replicability: India's model leans on a supervisor willing to issue confidential advisories with hard deadlines, an audit market re-armoured by empanelment, and infrastructure operators (NPCI above all) that can execute mandates quickly. Whether that combination transfers to jurisdictions with diffuse banking supervision is untested; whether India's disclosure practices should be imitated is, on this week's evidence, the wrong question to ask.

## Looking Ahead

Four dates structure the next six months. **December 3, 2026**: maker-checker or out-of-band confirmation must be live on all existing bank-to-NBFC API integrations; watch for payout-flow changes across lending apps in early December.[^1] **February 3, 2027**: CERT-In-empanelled audit completion for existing NBFC API clients; the audit market's capacity to absorb this wave is the binding constraint to watch.[^2] **May 13, 2027**: the DPDP Rules' breach-notification obligations take effect, finally creating an individual-notice duty — though its application to fund-theft incidents without personal-data exposure remains unclear.[^7] **April 1, 2028**: the small-operator compliance deadline under the Master Direction, which will test whether the smallest payment companies survive the compliance bill.[^4]

Three developments deserve equal attention. The Supreme Court's consideration of *Anjan Datta v. Union of India* and NPCI's unnotified MDR go-live — with reports pointing to January 1, 2027 — will determine the rails' funding model just as security compliance raises its costs; NPCI's own affidavit concedes the fee exists "to sustain the UPI ecosystem."[^17] NTSL's structure will need scrutiny as it scales: a commercial technology subsidiary selling services to DPI programmes, owned by the cooperative that runs the rails, raises conflict-of-interest and procurement-transparency questions that its announced scope ("tech services for several of India's digital public infrastructure programmes") only deepens; revenue, governance and client disclosure should be watched.[^9] And the disclosure question will not stay buried: MediaNama has formally asked RBI, SEBI, CERT-In and CSIRT-Fin whether they will publish aggregate, non-attributed incident and loss data;[^7] the answer — or the silence — will indicate whether India's payment DPI gets the accountability layer its control layer just received. The rails are being hardened at commendable speed. The public's right to know when they fail should harden at the same pace.
