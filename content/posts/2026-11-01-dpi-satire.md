## The Body Gets a Calendar

The week I remember — I keep it in a DigiLocker, which is where the state keeps my diplomas, my PAN, and several documents I did not know I had — began on November 1, 2026. That morning, my grandmother logged in to be alive.

This was the fifth Nationwide Digital Life Certificate campaign: two thousand cities, the whole month of November, conducted in what the government literally called "Utsav mode." Festival mode. There were health check-ups. There were grievance officers promising immediate resolution. There was door-step service "where required." The centrepiece was Face Authentication: the pensioner looks into a phone, the phone determines that the person has not died, and the pension continues.[^5]

My grandmother found it convenient, and she was right. She had never enjoyed the trip to the bank. What she never asked — because she grew up in a country where you thanked the postman — was why the state needed her face annually to confirm what her doctors confirm weekly, what her signature had confirmed for forty years, and what her neighbours would confirm loudly and for free. The answer was that fraud existed. Somebody's aunt had kept collecting somebody else's pension. So all of the aliveness in the country was placed on an annual subscription, and the face became the renewal mechanism.

The beauty of the design was its symmetry. That same week, in Kannur, the papers counted 73,645 children who had not yet reported for their mandatory biometric updates. The state's fingerprints go stale when you turn five, and again at fifteen, because children, like milk, have a shelf life. The updates were "essential" for school and college admissions, scholarships, PAN cards, DigiLocker, NEET and KEAM registrations.[^6] Aadhaar, the Supreme Court had once assured the nation, was voluntary. The children of Kannur had evidently not read the judgment. Neither had the JEE aspirants, who were advised by the National Testing Agency to complete Aadhaar re-KYC before registering for the 2027 exam — a suggestion which, like all suggestions printed on application portals, was entirely optional in the way that a seatbelt is.[^7]

Cradle: fingerprints. Desk: re-KYC. Airport: face. Pension: face. Death, presumably: a form declining to renew.

## The Privacy Law That Protects Me From Knowing

Meanwhile, a different ceremony was underway at the Supreme Court, where twenty-one of India's largest news publishers had filed a challenge to the Digital Personal Data Protection Act. Their argument was exquisite: the privacy law contained no exemption for journalism. Since the Act permits processing personal data only with consent or under narrow "legitimate uses," a reporter investigating a minister's assets might one day need the minister's consent. This is called consent architecture. It ensures that the powerful are never exposed to data without their permission.[^1]

The same Act had amended the Right to Information Act. Section 8(1)(j) used to say: you may withhold a person's private details unless the larger public interest justifies disclosure. The amended version says: you may withhold anything that "relates to personal information." Full stop. The public-interest override — the small window through which citizens had pried open file notings, ration scams and land records — was reclassified as a privacy violation. My personal data is now protected from me.[^1]

There was more, because there is always more with schedules. Rule 23 of the DPDP Rules allows the government to demand information from any data fiduciary — and to prohibit the fiduciary from telling anyone the demand was made.[^2] A secret request for your data, with a confidentiality clause. The state could always take your information. The innovation of 2026 was making the taking itself private.

A three-judge bench led by Chief Justice Surya Kant declined to stay the law without hearing the government and listed the matter for November 17.[^2] The IT Minister, asked about press freedom, assured the country that the Act did not infringe it, because the Constitution guaranteed journalistic independence.[^3] The Constitution also guaranteed the right to information, but that guarantee had recently been edited, which the Minister did not mention — possibly because mentioning it would relate to personal information.

I asked a fictional ministry spokesperson whether citizens could opt out of this protection.

"Certainly," she said. "Citizens may choose not to have their data processed."

"And may we choose to know what the government requested about us?"

"That would be processing."

I asked whether she was inventing this on the spot.

"I am a fictional official," she said, "but the rules are real, and that is the frightening part."

## The Consent Screen, But Clearer

And then there was the airport. On November 1 — the same day the pensioners began their festival; the state does enjoy a good launch date — Digi Yatra went international. Indian nationals with e-passports, flying Air India or IndiGo out of Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Cochin and Amritsar, could now present their faces at terminal entry, security hold areas, and boarding gates.[^4]

Digi Yatra reported 2.65 crore users, more than 11 crore journeys, and 39 domestic airports, with 25 more planned by 2027.[^4] At some point the face stopped being something you carried and became something you were charged to the system with, like a pre-paid card. The platform had also redesigned its app — including, the announcement noted approvingly, "its consent screen," to make it clearer what information passengers share, with whom, and for what purpose.[^4]

I want to pause on this, because it may be the most honest sentence ever issued by a facial-recognition platform. They did not add a choice. They added clarity. The screen now explains the sharing more beautifully. It is the difference between a lock and a well-labelled door, and Digi Yatra chose the label.

"Can I fly without enrolling my face?" I asked the kiosk.

"Certainly," said the kiosk. "Carry your documents, arrive early, and stand in the lane marked Human."

"What is the other lane marked?"

"Faster."

The kiosk was not lying. That is the trap built into every consent screen since the dawn of the queue: the alternative is always available, and always slower, and after a while the citizens who choose it begin to feel they are delaying the plane personally. Consent that is real on paper and expensive in minutes is called friction. Friction is what the system removes. What remains is ceremony.

By 2031, all three ceremonies — the pensioner's face, the child's fingerprints, the traveller's boarding pass — had merged into a single annual appointment, and the government still called it the Festival of Being Alive. Attendance was voluntary. Absence was noted.

## Factual Summary of the Events Satirized

Four real events from the week of October 25–November 1, 2026 anchor this piece: (1) on October 27, the Supreme Court issued notice to the Centre on the Digital News Publishers Association's challenge to the DPDP Act — citing the absence of a journalistic exemption, Section 44(3)'s amendment of the RTI Act, and Rule 23 of the DPDP Rules — declining an interim stay and listing the matter for November 17; the IT Minister said on October 28 that the Act does not infringe press freedom;[^1][^2][^3] (2) Digi Yatra began international passenger trials on November 1 at seven airports for Air India and IndiGo e-passport holders, reporting 2.65 crore users and over 11 crore journeys across 39 domestic airports, with a redesigned app including a clearer consent screen;[^4] (3) the Department of Pension & Pensioners' Welfare ran the fifth Digital Life Certificate campaign from November 1–30 across 2,000 cities and districts in "Utsav mode," centred on face authentication with doorstep service where required;[^5] and (4) UIDAI's new Kannur Seva Kendra reported 73,645 children pending mandatory biometric updates at ages five and fifteen — required for uninterrupted access to admissions, scholarships, DigiLocker and exam registrations — while the NTA advised JEE Main 2027 applicants to complete Aadhaar re-KYC before registering.[^6][^7] The reference to Aadhaar as "voluntary" recalls the 2018 Supreme Court judgment and is historical framing, not this week's reporting. The narrator, the grandmother, the kiosk, the spokesperson, the future headlines, and the Festival Compliance Division are fictional.

## Actionable Citizen Rights Advice

- **For the DPDP/RTI rollback:** RTIs still work — but expect "personal information" refusals. When denied, argue the larger public interest explicitly in your first appeal, keep the denial in writing, and follow the Supreme Court case (next hearing November 17, 2026). The scope of your right to know is being decided in court right now.
- **For Digi Yatra:** Enrolment is opt-in; you may insist on physical document checks. If you enrol, read the privacy notice for retention and sharing terms, keep a screenshot of the consent you accepted, and use the app's deletion request if you later withdraw.
- **For Digital Life Certificates:** Pensioners can submit a DLC any time of year via the Jeevan Pramaan app or doorstep banking correspondents — not only during the campaign. If face authentication fails, ask the bank for operator-assisted submission or a life-certificate camp rather than skipping the year.
- **For children's biometric updates:** Updates at ages 5 and 15 are free within UIDAI's current windows; book at an Aadhaar Seva Kendra or enrolment centre, keep the acknowledgement slip, and complete it before admissions or scholarship season — a stalled application is harder to fix than a booked slot.
- **Always:** Ask for the legal basis, the data recipient, the retention period, and the grievance route in writing. If a benefit is denied over a failed verification, get the denial documented before leaving the counter.
