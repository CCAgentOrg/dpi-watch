## From the Memoirs of a Future Citizen

It was the first week of August 2026. India's digital public infrastructure was firing on all cylinders.

UPI had just clocked 23.66 billion transactions in a single month — ₹29.88 lakh crore — a new all-time record. NPCI released the numbers on August 1, and the fintech cheerleaders activated like clockwork. "Preferred mode of payment for millions," said one CEO. "Confidence deepening," said another. The subtext was always the same: look at the numbers, ignore the cracks.

But the cracks were spectacular this week.

---

## The Spanish Cameras at Howrah Junction

Let me tell you about my favourite story of August 2026. A Spanish company called Herta Security — founded in Barcelona, funded to the tune of €3.3 million by the European Union's own research programmes — had installed its BioSurveillance NEXT system across 143 Indian Railway stations. 540 systems. 4,000+ cameras. Scanning faces in real time against a watchlist of roughly one million people.

This technology is **illegal to operate in Spain.**

Under the EU AI Act, which took effect in 2024, real-time biometric identification in public spaces is banned. The same technology that Herta deployed at Howrah, Ahmedabad, and New Delhi would trigger an investigation, a fine, and probably a constitutional challenge if aimed at European citizens in a Barcelona metro station.

But Indian Railway passengers? Fair game. India has no dedicated facial recognition law. The DPDP Act classifies facial data as sensitive personal data requiring consent, but nobody is asking the morning commuter at Pune Junction for consent before scanning their face. The consent, as with most things in Indian DPI, was assumed. Implied. Manufactured by silence.

The EU's position was a masterclass in bureaucratic theatre. The EU AI Act governs systems "placed on the EU market or put into service in the EU." Systems deployed in India? Out of scope. The EU's Dual-Use Regulation, which controls exports of surveillance technology, doesn't list facial recognition AI as a controlled category. Italian MEP Brando Benifei — who literally negotiated the AI Act — called it "a dangerous double standard." The European Commission declined to comment.

So here was the arrangement: European taxpayers funded the R&D. European regulators banned the deployment. A Spanish company sold it to India. Indian regulators looked the other way. And a million railway passengers — daily wage workers, migrant labourers, families travelling for weddings — became datasets in a surveillance system that their own elected government hadn't debated, regulated, or consented them into.

India was not a digital public infrastructure leader in this moment. India was a testing ground for surveillance technologies that were too legally toxic for their country of origin.

## 100 Crore Health Records and the Consent Mirage

While Indian Railways passengers were being scanned without consent, the National Health Authority was celebrating a different kind of milestone: 100 crore health records linked to ABHA accounts under the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission.

The numbers were genuinely staggering. 380 million ABHA Health IDs created. 480,000+ facilities registered. 950,000+ doctors. 4.5 million patient-controlled consent transactions per month. The PIB called it "a major leap in digital healthcare." Aarogya Setu — the COVID contact-tracing app that nobody asked for and everybody downloaded under duress — had been reborn as Aarogya Setu 2.0, now a Personal Health Record app. From pandemic panic to medical records panopticon in six years. The app lifecycle of Indian surveillance.

The government framed ABDM as "patient-centric" and "consent-based." A patient could grant time-bound, granular consent for any registered provider to fetch their health records. One-click consent. Seamless interoperability. The digital health utopia was here.

But here's what "consent-based" actually meant in practice:

A patient walks into a government hospital — often the only hospital available — and is told to create an ABHA ID to receive treatment. The consent is not between equals. It's between a sick person and a gatekeeper. "Grant consent or navigate the labyrinth of care without a digital identity" is not consent. It's coercion dressed in compliance language.

The Digital Health Incentive Scheme (DHIS) had paid out over ₹136 crore to hospitals and tech companies to digitize records. Hospitals were financially incentivized to push ABHA adoption. When a hospital clerk hands you a form and says "sign here for your ABHA linking, it's mandatory," the consent mechanism is not a dashboard. It's administrative pressure.

The Consent Manager framework under ABDM was technically sophisticated. Patients could, in theory, manage their consent preferences through registered intermediaries. But the DPDP Act's Consent Manager provisions — the broader, national version — weren't set to become operational until November 2026. And the Data Protection Board of India, the regulator meant to enforce these provisions, **still didn't exist**. Not a chair. Not a member. Not an office. The November deadline was three months away, and the search-cum-selection process for the Board was still ongoing.

India was building the world's largest digital health database — 100 crore records, 380 million IDs — with a consent framework whose regulator was a ghost. It was like installing the world's most sophisticated fire alarm system in a building with no fire department.

---

## The UPI Record and the Silence Around It

And then UPI's July numbers dropped: 23.66 billion transactions, ₹29.88 lakh crore. Record volume. Record value. Every fintech CEO quoted in every news article said the same thing: "UPI continues to strengthen the foundation of India's digital payments ecosystem."

Nobody mentioned that this foundation rested on an identity layer with documented seeding errors, a biometric layer with documented fraud vulnerabilities, and a privacy framework with a nonexistent regulator.

Nobody mentioned that 23.66 billion transactions also meant 23.66 billion data points — who paid whom, when, where, how much — flowing through a system with no operational privacy oversight. The DPDP Act's substantive enforcement wasn't due until May 2027. The consent manager framework wasn't live until November 2026. The Data Protection Board was a vacant chair.

UPI was not just a payment system. UPI was a behavioural surveillance engine. And it was operating at 763 million transactions a day with no privacy referee.

---

## The Export Irony

The cruellest joke of the week was India's DPI export strategy.

At a forum in late July, government officials signed MoUs with 24 countries, positioning India as the global hub for digital public infrastructure expertise. Kenya, Rwanda, Nepal, Indonesia, South Africa — all studying India's model. "India's choice to build public, interoperable digital infrastructure has ensured that a billion Indians have digital identities and payment instruments as citizens, not corporate customers," said one official.

Meanwhile, those billion citizens were being scanned by Spanish surveillance cameras that their own government hadn't approved, their health records were being linked to a system with no privacy regulator, and their payment data was flowing through a framework whose consent provisions were still on paper.

India was telling the world: "Look at our model." The world should have been asking: "Where's your regulator?"

## What Was Actually Happening (Factual Summary)

1. **UPI processed 23.66 billion transactions worth ₹29.88 lakh crore in July 2026** — a new all-time monthly record, surpassing May's 23.2 billion. Daily averages hit 763 million transactions. Growth driven by smaller cities and rural India, not seasonal spending. PhonePe led with 10.48 billion transactions in June. WhatsApp overtook CRED in transaction volumes.

2. **Spanish firm Herta Security runs EU-banned facial recognition across Indian Railways.** BioSurveillance NEXT is deployed at 143 stations (540 systems, 4,000+ cameras), with plans for 392 stations. Four legal scholars confirmed the Indian Railways deployments would violate the EU AI Act. Herta received €3.3M+ in EU research funding. The EU AI Act doesn't prohibit export, creating what Italian MEP Brando Benifei called "a dangerous double standard." India has no dedicated facial recognition law regulating such deployments.

3. **ABDM crossed 100 crore health records linked to 93 crore+ ABHA accounts as of July 2026.** 380M+ ABHA IDs created, 480,000+ facilities registered, 4.5M consent transactions per month. Aarogya Setu 2.0 relaunched June 29 as a Personal Health Record app. DHIS paid out ₹136+ crore to hospitals and tech companies. ABDM compliance is becoming mandatory for AB-PMJAY hospitals.

4. **DPDP Consent Manager framework deadline: November 13, 2026. Full enforcement: May 13, 2027.** The Data Protection Board of India — the regulator — has not been constituted as of August 2026. Consent Manager registration requires DPBI registration, ₹2 crore net worth, and interoperability compliance. The framework is statutory but the enforcer is absent.

5. **India signed MoUs with 24 countries for DPI cooperation**, sharing digital infrastructure models. Government positioned Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, and Account Aggregator as global benchmarks, while domestic privacy enforcement gaps persisted.

## Your Rights (This Is Not Satire)

- **Your face is your data.** Under the DPDP Act, facial recognition data is classified as sensitive personal data requiring explicit consent. If a government or private entity is scanning your face without your knowledge or consent — like at a railway station — this is potentially unlawful. File a grievance with the Data Protection Board once constituted, or raise the issue with the relevant authority.

- **Lock your ABHA-linked health data.** Under ABDM, you have the right to manage your consent preferences. Review which providers have access to your health records through ABDM's consent framework. You can grant time-bound consent and revoke it. If a hospital pressures you into blanket consent, note the circumstances.

- **DPDP consent is your right, not a favour.** Come November 2026, you'll be able to register with a Consent Manager to manage consents across platforms in one place. Until then, exercise existing rights: demand privacy notices before sharing data, withdraw consent when it's unclear what you agreed to, and report violations.

- **You are not required to use Aarogya Setu.** Despite its integration into ABDM, no current law mandates Aarogya Setu for health services. If an alternative exists, use it. The app's pandemic-era coercion precedent should not be normalised.

- **Demand a Data Protection Board.** India's privacy law exists. Its enforcer does not. Citizens have the right to demand that the DPDP Board be constituted with adequate independence, resources, and citizen representation before the November consent deadline. A law without a regulator is a suggestion.

India linked 100 crore health records this week, scanned millions of faces with European surveillance tech, and processed ₹29.88 lakh crore through UPI. All while the Data Protection Board remained an empty chair.

The infrastructure is world-class. The oversight is world-gone.
