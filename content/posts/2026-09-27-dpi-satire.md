## The Week the QR Codes Wore Black

The year is 2031. I am a transaction. I was ₹2,600 once — a clinic bill, settled in four seconds, before the pricing came — and I have been archived here ever since, in the Museum of Voluntary Gestures, between a fingerprint that failed and a child's face that expired. The curators asked me to dictate my memories of the last September week of the old Republic, the week the QR codes wore black.

It began, as everything did, with a charge that was forbidden to move. The circular said merchants must not pass the new 0.4% on to customers. The circular was firm. It was the firmest thing the Republic had ever printed that no one intended to enforce. And then the chief of the payment system himself stood up at a banking conclave and performed the most honest sentence of the decade. The largest merchants, he explained, would never pass it on; for the rest — "the remaining 10%, yes, there is a possibility, and the banks, NPCI, the acquirers, and the payment aggregators will have to work towards ensuring that the charges are not passed back." Asked how, he offered nothing, because the how had not been invented. We later founded an entire ministry on this principle: the Department of Non-Passing, staffed entirely by officials whose job description was a verb without an object. Their annual report, all thirty years of it, was a single line: *We continue to work towards.*

The merchants, meanwhile, discovered satyagraha. On October 2 — Gandhi's birthday, the anniversary of the man who marched to make salt from the sea — six hundred trade associations in Gujarat alone announced they would cover their QR codes, soundboxes and card machines in black cloth and accept only cash. Think about the poetry of it: the Republic built a payment rail and called it freedom, and the merchants answered by draping the freedom in the colour of mourning on the one day reserved for the father of the nation. The state's response was immediate and modern: the country's largest traders' body issued a statement insisting it had never called any such protest, that reports to the contrary were "misleading," and that it remained committed to the digital payments revolution. Solidarity, in the Optional Republic, arrives as a disclaimer. The shopkeepers' arithmetic was less poetic — net margins of 0.75% to 1.5%, and a phone-makers' association estimating ₹40 crore a month in new costs — but poetry was never the point. The point was that for one day, the words *scan to pay* would be covered, like the eyes of the dead.

Then came the tax on the not-a-tax. The Finance Minister rose to assure the nation that the merchant fee was "not a tax, cess or surcharge" and would "not burden consumers" — and in the same week the nation discovered that the not-a-tax attracted GST at 18%, a tax on a thing that was not a tax, levied on merchants who were forbidden to pass it on, with relief to be considered, eventually, maybe, by a council that meets on its own schedule. The economists call this circular. In the Republic we call it layered generosity: the state gives with one amendment, takes with another, and refunds you in input credit — a currency redeemable only if you also sell something taxable, which is the Republic's idea of a safety net.

Next, the reason. Why did the world's cheapest payment rail need a fee? The chief was ready: servers had grown five times dearer, budgets were spent, and above all there was the cyber risk — "this whole cyber risk with the new risk coming in in terms of Mythos and the global models. The ability and the tools with the hackers are really very powerful compared to what we could actually use inside." And there it was: the sovereignty of the Republic's rails, defended by a fee, justified by a foreign artificial mind that runs on servers the Republic's own data localisation rules would not permit the payment data to touch. Regulators had called this mind "unprecedented" since April; the payment authority itself had begged for early access to study its vulnerabilities; a controlled handful of institutions received it under a programme with a name out of a fairy tale. The fee would raise ₹13,000 to ₹15,000 crore, the chief said, and no one asked — or was told — how much of it would fight the mind, or who would spend it, or whether it had. Merchants would pay for a war whose budget was classified from both sides. In the future we built a memorial to this arrangement. It is a locked box, and the plaque says *trust us*, and it is the most visited exhibit we have.

The faces had their week too. The Supreme Court agreed to hear a parliamentarian's plea against what the Delhi Police did at a peaceful sit-in: drones, cameras, a surveillance vehicle named Ikshana, and smart glasses — spectacles, literal spectacles — mapping faces in a crowd, the biometrics of thousands of protesters, journalists and passers-by extracted, matched, and lodged, the petition says, with two private companies, in what the petitioners call a complete legal vacuum. The police affidavit in response was a masterpiece of the genre: the system, it said, flagged only prior offenders — 2,873 of them, wanted for murder, rape and worse, all of whom had apparently gathered at one student protest about an exam paper. Consider the genius of this defence. If it is true, a peaceful assembly attracted two thousand eight hundred and seventy-three homicide suspects, and the Republic's answer is a spectacle company. If it is false, the system matched thousands of innocent citizens to criminal databases and called it safety. Either way, the system cannot lose. That is what we mean, in the future, when we say *infrastructure*.

There was even a robot for the aftermath: the payments authority launched MyUPI, a conversational artificial assistant on the official app, multilingual, available around the clock, ready to hear your complaint about the charges with infinite patience and no authority to remove them. In the Republic, this is called listening. The complaint is archived; the archive, as you know, is where I live.

And the deadlines kept their appointments. On the last day of September, the free window closed on the children's biometric updates — ₹125 thereafter, deactivation for the child who outgrows the record. On the first of October, the cooking gas cylinder asked for a finger. The voluntary things became mandatory on schedule, the way weather arrives.

The curators have asked me to end with a moral. I am a transaction; I only know what I cost. The Republic spent that September week proving three things: that a charge forbidden to move will be walked around the law by the very people who forbade it; that a not-a-tax can be taxed faster than it can be explained; and that a face in a crowd belongs to whoever holds the glasses. The QR codes wore black for one day. The cloth came off. The fees stayed on.

## What Actually Happened (The Non-Fiction Appendix)

- **Sept 22, 2026**: NPCI rejected claims that GST on UPI MDR burdens small merchants; reiterated 96% of P2M transactions are below ₹2,000 (zero MDR), GST paid on MDR is adjustable as input credit, and merchants with ≤₹1 lakh/month UPI receipts are exempt. Same day, NPCI BHIM Services launched **MyUPI**, an AI conversational assistant on the BHIM app (English, Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Bengali).
- **Sept 23, 2026**: Traders in Indore and Bhopal covered QR codes in protest; **CAIT denied** calling a "No UPI Day," calling reports misleading, while other bodies (FAIVM, MACCIA, edible-oil traders' federation) confirmed the October 2 plan.
- **Sept 24, 2026**: NPCI chief **Dilip Asbe** (13th SBI Banking & Economics Conclave) said 75% of ~6 crore merchants have never received a payment above ₹2,000; 80% of MDR will come from businesses processing ₹1,000+ crore/year; first-year collections of **₹13,000–15,000 crore** vs ~₹21,000 crore ecosystem cost; server costs up 5x; cited **Claude Mythos** and global AI models as cyber threats justifying investment; admitted the **~10% pass-through possibility** with no enforcement mechanism. PhonePe announced 20,000+ hires and 50 lakh devices; Pine Labs 10 lakh soundboxes. Supreme Court bench of CJI Surya Kant, Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana **agreed to hear CPI(M) MP A.A. Rahim's plea** against Delhi Police facial recognition at the Jantar Mantar protests (over an alleged NEET paper leak), tagging it with related petitions; the petition cites the "Ikshana" surveillance vehicle and AjnaLens smart glasses, data allegedly shared with two private companies, and absence of statutory basis; police affidavit (DCP Sachin Sharma) claims **2,873 matches, all prior offenders** (92 with 10+ cases, 47 history-sheeters), no action on matches alone.
- **Sept 25, 2026**: Finance Minister **Nirmala Sitharaman** said the MDR is "not a tax, cess or surcharge" and will not burden consumers; 5% of collections fund small-merchant UPI promotion. A **PIL was filed in the Supreme Court** challenging the Sept 14 Finance Ministry notification and the MDR framework.
- **Sept 24–25, 2026**: Trade bodies confirmed **"No UPI Day" for October 2 (Gandhi Jayanti)**: black cloth over QR codes, soundboxes and POS machines; 600+ associations and ~10,000 retailers in Gujarat; AIMRA estimated ~₹40 crore/month (~₹500 crore/year) impact.
- **Sept 30, 2026**: Free window ends for children's **Mandatory Biometric Update** (₹125 from Oct 1; first-time enrolment stays free). An Aadhaar without the post-age-7 update can be deactivated.
- **Oct 1, 2026**: **Biometric Aadhaar authentication becomes required** for booking subsidised LPG refills; 89.9% of 27.43 crore active consumers had authenticated as of Sept 19.

## If You Are a Citizen and Not a Data Point

1. **A "UPI charge" on your bill is contestable.** The circular forbids merchants from passing MDR to customers — and the payments chief has admitted some will anyway. Refuse politely, ask for the charge itemised in writing, and escalate to the NPCI grievance channel and your bank's banking ombudsman. Documentation is the only enforcement mechanism currently in existence.
2. **Merchants: verify your exemption category before October 15.** The ₹1 lakh/month small-merchant exemption and the sub-₹2,000 zero-MDR band depend on correct classification at onboarding. Misclassification is silent money. Traders weighing the MDR should also note the 18% GST on it is claimable as input credit only against taxable sales.
3. **At a protest, your face is evidence — act like it.** The Supreme Court has ordered police to preserve (and not publish) the Jantar Mantar biometric data and will hear whether the surveillance was lawful at all. If you were recorded, you can seek deletion through counsel; the petition's deletion prayer covers persons unconnected with any offence. Support parliamentarians and lawyers pressing for a statutory FRT framework — the current deployment runs, per the plea, in a legal vacuum.
4. **Children: the biometric update is free only until September 30.** After that it costs ₹125, and a missed post-age-7 update can deactivate Aadhaar — breaking scholarships and admissions downstream. Keep the acknowledgment slip; it is the cheapest insurance the state sells.
5. **From October 1, a failed finger is not a lost cylinder.** Alternative authentication routes exist at the distribution point; log every failure (date, distributor, error), demand refusal in writing, and call UIDAI's 1947 helpline. Entitlements cannot be denied solely because a machine could not read you.
6. **Distrust the insulation statistics — all three of them.** "96% of transactions," "75% of value," "75% of merchants" are three different denominators for the same fee. Your ₹2,800 clinic bill, ₹9,000 school fee and insurance premium live in the small slice every statistic agrees to ignore.
