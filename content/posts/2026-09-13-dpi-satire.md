## The Week Your Face Joined the Workforce

Let me tell you about the September when the face was nationalised. The year is 2032 as I write this, from inside a verification queue that began when I was thirty. In 2026, at something called the Global Fintech Fest — a four-day interval in which Mumbai's hotels achieve full biometric saturation — the Unique Identification Authority announced that your face would now live inside every app.

The press release called it a Face Authentication SDK. What it means is this: until that week, a bank verifying your face summoned a separate government app, and you performed aliveness on camera like a hostage proof-of-life video. The SDK removed the extra app. The face is now native — embedded, a library call. Friction, we were told, was the enemy. Nobody asked what friction had been protecting.

The numbers were presented as trophies: **five billion face transactions** since 2021, across some **two hundred entities** — banks, insurers, brokers, telecom operators. Face authentication was introduced to prove pensioners were alive so their pensions could continue. It was the humane option. Then it learned e-KYC, welfare queues, SIM cards. By 2026 it had a "Playbook on Ease of Onboarding" — a document teaching institutions how to install your face with minimal fuss. In the future we received a similar playbook. It had one page. The page said: open.

Here is what I want the record to show: the same week the SDK launched, a video from Nagaland went viral. A Naga citizen sat before a KYC liveness check and the scanner declined him. Again. Again. The machine wanted him to blink "naturally." His countrymen — this is not satire, though it rhymes with it — stood around him holding his eyelids open so the camera could confirm he was alive. It was shared, gently, by a Nagaland minister, as an anecdote of struggle. It was a thesis statement. The system that could not confirm the aliveness of a living citizen was, that same week, celebrating five billion confirmations and announcing its global expansion.

Because that was the other announcement. The CEO of UIDAI, at the same festival, said the quiet plan out loud: Aadhaar would go **global**. For the NRIs and OCIs. Officials noted our architects already advise in twenty-nine countries; a World Bank knowledge hub would carry "India's experience" onward. Experience is a generous word. The ID sworn to the Supreme Court as voluntary had become, in sixteen years, an export commodity — a package of protocols, liveness thresholds, and denial statistics with a flag on it.

And where, you ask — because in the future we are still encouraged to ask — was consent? Consent was being scheduled. The DPDP Act, our great privacy constitution, had its rules notified in November 2025, with the Consent Manager framework — the machinery that would let a person see and withdraw what they agreed to — switched on in November 2026, and full obligations by May 2027. The face scans shipped in September. Do you see the architecture? **Deployment first, consent later.** Five billion face transactions occurred before consent infrastructure existed to govern one of them, and the Data Protection Board itself was still being assembled — a crew hired for a ship already at sea.

I do not blame the engineers. The liveness detection is genuinely clever; the anti-spoofing is real. That is what makes it tragedy instead of incompetence: the system works precisely well enough to remove every pause in which a citizen might have asked a question.

Even ONDC, our open commerce utopia, appeared that week: an "intent to explore collaboration" with Visa, for tourism. Note the grammar. Not a partnership. Not a pilot. An *intent to explore an intent*. In the same month ONDC celebrated five hundred million cumulative transactions — a number UPI clears many times over every day, at twenty-four and a half billion a month. Announcements use the future tense. Achievements use someone else's numbers.

## If You Are a Citizen and Not a Data Point

1. **A failed face scan is not a lawful denial.** Welfare entitlements cannot be denied solely because biometric authentication failed — the Aadhaar regulations and courts require alternative means (OTP authentication, offline verification). If a pension or ration is denied on a failed scan, demand the exception in writing.
2. **Use the alternatives that exist today.** Jeevan Pramaan life certificates, Aadhaar OTP authentication, and offline XML e-KYC are lawful substitutes for face or fingerprint scans. Insist on them; operators default to biometrics because it is cheaper for them, not safer for you.
3. **Log every authentication failure.** Timestamp, app, error code, UIDAI helpline (1947) reference. When Consent Managers go live on 13 November 2026 and the Data Protection Board functions, a documented failure trail converts your inconvenience into somebody's liability.
4. **Aadhaar remains legally voluntary for most private services.** The Supreme Court's Puttaswamy judgment barred mandatory Aadhaar KYC by private companies. An SDK in every app makes refusal socially harder; the law still makes it possible. Insist, in writing.
5. **When an announcement uses the future tense, file it under fiction** until rules, dates, and a working grievance channel exist. "Intent to explore" is a mood, not infrastructure.
