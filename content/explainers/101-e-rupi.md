# e-RUPI 101: India's Purpose-Bound Digital Voucher

## What is e-RUPI?

**e-RUPI** is a prepaid digital voucher delivered to a beneficiary as an SMS string or QR code and redeemable for a specified service at an enabled provider. NPCI built it on UPI with government departments and banks. It is **not** a bank transfer into the beneficiary's account, a general-purpose wallet, or the Reserve Bank's digital rupee (e₹): the voucher is meant to pay an authorised provider for a defined purpose. [^1][^2][^3]

Its policy appeal is straightforward: a sponsor can fund a benefit for an intended person and purpose without requiring the recipient to hold a bank account, payment app, or internet banking. The corresponding trade-off is equally important: the recipient gets less choice over where and how the value can be spent than with unrestricted cash. e-RUPI is therefore best understood as a **digitised, restricted benefit**, not simply “cashless DBT.” [^2]

### Historical context

NPCI developed e-RUPI with the Department of Financial Services, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, and the National Health Authority. The Union government launched it on **2 August 2021**, initially highlighting health services and COVID-19 vaccination. At launch, the government said 11 banks were partnered and more than 1,600 hospitals could redeem vouchers; those are launch-era figures, not current network totals. [^2][^3]

The rules later broadened. In February 2022 the RBI proposed allowing government vouchers up to ₹1 lakh and multiple redemptions until their value was used. In June 2023 it proposed permitting non-bank PPI issuers and voucher issuance on behalf of individuals. Subsequent RBI reporting records a wider set of use cases and revised limits. This evolution means the early description of e-RUPI as invariably “one-time” is no longer accurate for every voucher type. [^4][^5][^6][^7]

## How it works

1. **A sponsor defines the benefit.** A government department, employer, or other eligible sponsor sets the programme's intended purpose, eligible beneficiaries, participating providers, value, and conditions. RBI has also enabled individual-use cases through its later policy changes. [^3][^6]
2. **An authorised issuer creates the voucher.** A participating bank—or, under the expanded framework, an authorised non-bank PPI issuer—creates the voucher under applicable operating rules. The sponsor supplies or verifies the information needed to direct it to the intended recipient. [^6][^7]
3. **The recipient receives a code.** The voucher is sent to a mobile number as an SMS string or QR code. The original design did not require the recipient to have a bank account, card, payment app, or internet banking. [^2]
4. **The recipient uses it with an enabled provider.** At a participating hospital, shop, or other provider, the recipient presents the code. The provider checks and redeems it under that programme's rules. The sponsor determines which providers and purposes qualify; the voucher is not spendable everywhere UPI is accepted. [^2][^3]
5. **The provider is paid.** The launch description says payment to the service provider follows completion of the transaction. Depending on the voucher category, it may be single-use, multi-redemption, reloadable, or non-reloadable. The voucher's issuer and scheme terms—not the word “e-RUPI” alone—determine the applicable conditions. [^2][^5][^7]

### Two official descriptions that need reconciling

The 2021 launch material and NPCI's product page describe e-RUPI as a one-time-use voucher. Later RBI policy expanded government vouchers to permit multiple redemptions and reloads, and corporate vouchers to permit reloading; individual-use vouchers were separately described as non-reloadable. The later, use-case-specific rules qualify the older, simplified “one-time” description. Users should check the terms on their own voucher rather than assume every voucher expires after one redemption or can be reused. [^1][^5][^7]

There is a similar change in issuer descriptions: early product material describes bank issuance, while RBI later permitted authorised non-bank PPI issuers. NPCI's public-facing product copy may lag these changes. For a live voucher, identify the named issuer and the programme terms shown on the message or provider receipt. [^1][^6][^7]

The RBI's consolidated PPI Master Directions were updated on **30 September 2026**, but the public directions do not provide a single consumer-facing table covering every e-RUPI category. Check the specific issuer's current terms before relying on an older limit. [^7][^9]

## Layers classification (L1–L7)

This is DPI Watch's analytical mapping, not an official RBI or NPCI classification.

| Layer | e-RUPI role |
|---|---|
| **L1 — Policy and purpose** | A welfare department, employer, or other sponsor defines the entitlement and its conditions. [^2][^8] |
| **L2 — Regulation and accountability** | RBI regulates payment-system and PPI activity under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007; NPCI sets and operates the relevant UPI product arrangements. [^6][^9] |
| **L3 — Digital rails** | NPCI's UPI ecosystem supports issuance and redemption flows between issuers and participating providers. [^1][^3] |
| **L4 — Protocol and instrument** | The voucher is represented as a mobile-delivered SMS string or QR code and is redeemed under product and scheme rules. [^1][^2] |
| **L5 — Applications and access points** | Issuer channels, merchant/provider systems, and—in some programmes—consumer apps mediate access. An app is not a universal e-RUPI requirement, but a particular programme can choose an app-based journey. [^2][^10] |
| **L6 — Services and use cases** | Health, government benefits, employee welfare, and other purpose-bound services are possible; the sponsor's rules determine where a voucher works. [^2][^8] |
| **L7 — Reporting and analytics** | Issuers and programme administrators can observe issuance and redemption events. NPCI describes voucher programmes as enabling administrators to track use; this creates a public-interest case for aggregated reporting as well as privacy safeguards. [^1] |

## Regulatory framework

- **Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007:** RBI's Master Directions on Prepaid Payment Instruments are issued under sections 18 and 10(2) of the Act. RBI authorises and supervises relevant payment-system and PPI issuers. [^9]
- **RBI's PPI Master Directions:** The current consolidated directions cover banks, non-bank PPI issuers, and system participants. They set issuer authorisation, customer-service, record-keeping, and security requirements. RBI's 2023 policy changes and later annual reporting explain how e-RUPI issuance expanded beyond its initial bank-only model. [^6][^7][^9]
- **Transaction records:** RBI's PPI directions require PPI issuers to retain transaction logs for at least ten years and to preserve customer-information confidentiality. For a voucher issued through the PPI framework, ask the issuer which voucher identifiers and personal details its records contain and how the retention rule applies to that specific programme. [^9]
- **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and Rules, 2025:** The Act and Rules have a phased commencement. The official commencement schedule notified on 13 November 2025 places the core data-processing duties and individual rights in the 18-month tranche—scheduled for **13 May 2027**. As of this explainer's date, 8 October 2026, it would be premature to describe the Act's full set of rights as already enforceable. The precise provisions in force depend on the notification's staged dates. [^11][^12]
- **Scheme rules still matter:** A law governing payment issuers does not itself make every voucher a general entitlement or guarantee redemption at every provider. Programme eligibility, participating outlets, validity, reissue, and complaint routes should be disclosed by the sponsor and issuer. [^2][^9]

## Citizen rights analysis

### What can help

- **Lower entry requirements:** The launch design did not require the recipient to hold a bank account, card, payment app, or internet banking; it still depends on a working mobile phone and a participating provider. That is a lower entry hurdle, not universal access. [^2]
- **Purpose protection:** A voucher restricted to a defined service can reduce the chance that programme funds are spent outside the sponsor's stated purpose. This is a design capability, not proof that a programme is leak-proof or that every beneficiary can find an adequate provider. [^1][^2]
- **Provider payment assurance:** Prepayment can give a service provider greater assurance that the sponsor has funded the benefit. [^2]

### What deserves scrutiny

- **Choice is constrained by design.** The same restriction that makes a voucher purpose-bound can limit a recipient to participating providers, locations, or goods. If the nearest provider is unavailable, out of stock, inaccessible, or unsuitable, the voucher's nominal value may not equal usable support. This is a design risk to test in each scheme, not a measured national failure rate. [^2]
- **Phone delivery is not universal accessibility.** SMS delivery reduces reliance on a smartphone or mobile-data app, but still depends on access to a working phone/SIM, readable messages, network coverage, and a participating provider. Some implementations can add app requirements: NPCI's 2024 PM Vishwakarma announcement said artisans could access that programme's e-RUPI vouchers exclusively through the BHIM app. This is a programme-specific choice, not a technical rule for every e-RUPI voucher. [^2][^10]
- **A voucher is not cash.** The launch materials describe a prepaid instrument redeemable for specified services; they do not establish a universal right to convert an unused or expired voucher into cash. Ask about expiry, partial use, provider refusal, lost-phone replacement, and reissue before accepting a voucher as the only route to a benefit. [^2][^5][^7]
- **A programme count is not an outcome measure.** The government reported use across 13+ schemes, but the official references reviewed did not provide a consolidated national total for vouchers issued, redeemed, expired, failed, or left unused. Without those figures, scheme coverage alone cannot establish effective access or reduced leakage. [^8]

## Privacy implications

An e-RUPI voucher is delivered to a mobile number and used at an enabled provider. **Operational inference:** someone in the issuer/sponsor chain must link the voucher to its intended recipient for delivery and redemption; that follows from the person-specific, mobile-delivery design, but does not prove that every participant sees a named identity. NPCI says programme administrators can track use. These facts do **not** establish that every participant sees the same personal data or that the payment system is universally anonymous. [^1][^2]

The 2021 PIB explainer said redemption need not require the recipient to share personal details at the point of service. That is narrower than end-to-end anonymity: the voucher must be delivered to a phone, and the sponsor may hold the mapping between the voucher and an enrolled beneficiary. Public-facing material reviewed for this explainer does not provide a complete, e-RUPI-specific data-flow map showing which fields each actor receives, who can link redemption to a named person, or how scheme-side linkage records are governed. RBI separately requires PPI issuers to retain PPI transaction logs for at least ten years. Treat the remaining questions as transparency gaps—not proof of a particular hidden data practice. [^2][^9]

Purpose-bound vouchers can also reveal sensitive facts through context. For example, redemption at a health provider or under a treatment-specific scheme could disclose something about a person's health if the sponsor or issuer can link the transaction to the beneficiary. That is a **linkability risk**, not a finding that every provider receives a diagnosis or clinical record. The voucher should carry only the minimum information needed to authorise payment, and programme reporting should use aggregate data that cannot identify recipients. [^1][^2]

As of 8 October 2026, the DPDP Act's core data-processing provisions and individual rights are on the notified delayed timetable described above. Do not rely on a generic claim that “DPDP consent” already guarantees a recipient's control over every e-RUPI data flow; request the issuer's privacy notice and the programme's data-sharing, retention, and grievance terms. [^11][^12]

## Safeguards

**For recipients:**

- Before using a voucher, confirm the issuer, amount, eligible purpose and provider, validity date, whether partial or repeat redemption is allowed, and what happens to unused value.
- Ask the sponsor how to replace or block a voucher if the phone/SIM is lost, the message is deleted, or a provider refuses a valid redemption.
- Treat the SMS/QR code as value-bearing. Do not forward it, post it publicly, or hand it to someone who is not processing the stated service. Keep the voucher message and any receipt until the programme confirms successful redemption.
- Ask whether the scheme requires Aadhaar or another identity document; do not assume a universal identity requirement from the e-RUPI name alone.
- If a voucher is the only way to receive an essential benefit, ask the sponsor for a phone-assisted, in-person, or other accessible fallback and an appeal route.

**For sponsors and issuers:**

- Publish plain-language terms in relevant local languages, including validity, provider list, partial use, reissue, fees (if any), data sharing, retention, and complaint escalation.
- Offer a non-app pathway wherever the scheme promises basic-phone access; test it with people with disabilities, low literacy, unreliable network access, and shared or changed phones.
- Preserve an accessible fallback for failed redemption and a clear appeal process for eligibility or provider disputes.
- Publish privacy-protective aggregate counts for issued, redeemed, expired, failed, and reissued vouchers, with meaningful coverage breakdowns and a clear methodology.

These are recommended safeguards, not a claim that every programme already provides them.

## Complaints and grievance redressal

1. **Start with the issuer and sponsor.** Use the grievance contact shown in the voucher or issuer's website. For a payment, delivery, or redemption failure, give the voucher/reference number, date, provider, and the remedy sought. For eligibility or a welfare-scheme decision, also complain to the sponsoring department; RBI cannot decide every programme-policy dispute. [^9][^13]
2. **Escalate a regulated-entity service failure to RBI where covered.** The RBI's Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2026 has applied since 1 July 2026 and covers specified entities, including non-bank PPI issuers. First complain to the entity. If the entity does not respond within 30 days (or a longer applicable RBI/NPCI/Card Network period) or you are dissatisfied with its response, the RBI FAQ says a complaint may be filed within 90 days of the relevant deadline or last communication. [^13]
3. **File through RBI's channels.** Use the [RBI Complaint Management System](https://cms.rbi.org.in), email `crpc@rbi.org.in`, or submit a physical complaint to RBI's Centralised Receipt and Processing Centre. The RBI lists PPI complaint contacts and issuer grievance links on its complaints pages. [^13][^14]
4. **Check coverage before filing.** The Ombudsman route concerns a covered regulated entity's deficiency in service; a complaint against a government sponsor or an unregulated merchant may need to go through the relevant scheme or service-provider grievance channel instead. [^13]
